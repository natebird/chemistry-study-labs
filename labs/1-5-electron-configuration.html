2. Key ideas

F ∝ q₁q₂ / r²

Coulomb's law

The attraction between the nucleus and an electron grows with the nuclear charge and falls quickly with distance. Double the distance and the force drops to one quarter. Opposite charges attract; like charges repel.

Aufbau order

Filling order

Electrons fill the lowest-energy subshells first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p. Each s subshell holds 2 electrons, each p holds 6 (3 orbitals), and each d holds 10 (5 orbitals).

Pauli and Hund

Filling orbitals

An orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins (Pauli). In a set of equal-energy orbitals, electrons go in one per orbital before any pair up (Hund), which is why N has three unpaired 2p electrons.

Core vs. valence

Shielding

Core electrons sit closer to the nucleus and shield the outer electrons from part of its charge. Valence electrons are farther away and feel less attraction, so they are the ones involved in bonding and the easiest to remove.

4s out first

Ions

Cations lose electrons from the highest n first, so transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d: Fe is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ and Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶. Anions add electrons to the next open subshell: Cl⁻ is [Ar], isoelectronic with argon.

Not tested

Cr and Cu

Chromium ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵) and copper ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰) break the Aufbau pattern. The CED says configurations of these exceptions are not assessed on the exam, but it is useful to recognize them.

4. Configurations and Coulomb's law on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the complete configuration when asked for it (1s² 2s² …); use noble-gas shorthand only when the question allows it.
  • For a Coulomb's law explanation, name both factors: the charge (number of protons, or ion charge) and the distance (which shell the electron is in).
  • Compare the two species directly: "Mg has more protons than Na, and its valence electrons are in the same shell (n = 3), so they are attracted more strongly."
  • Mention shielding by core electrons when the electrons being compared are in different shells.
  • For transition-metal cations, show the 4s electrons removed first.
  • Use "isoelectronic" correctly: same number of electrons, same configuration (O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺).

Common mistakes

  • Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming a transition-metal cation.
  • Pairing electrons in a p or d subshell before each orbital has one (breaking Hund's rule).
  • Counting the 3d¹⁰ electrons of Ga–Kr as valence electrons. Valence electrons are in the highest n shell.
  • Saying "the electrons are more stable" or "the atom wants a full octet" instead of using charge and distance.
  • Using only "more shielding" to explain a trend within a period. Across a period the shielding by core electrons stays about the same; the nuclear charge increases.
  • Forgetting that force falls with the square of the distance: doubling r cuts the force to ¼, not ½.
