Hess’s Law Study Lab

If a reaction can be written as the sum of other reactions, its ΔH is the sum of their ΔH values. To use that, you reverse and multiply the given equations until they add up to the one you want, and do the same things to their ΔH values. Solve the puzzles below and watch the species cancel.

2. Key ideas

The law

Steps add, so their ΔH values add

Many processes can be broken into a series of steps, each with its own ΔH. Because energy is conserved, the total heat transferred in the series equals the sum of the heats for each step, so ΔHoverall = ΔH1 + ΔH2 + …

Rule 1

Reverse: flip the sign

If A → B has ΔH = −50 kJ, then B → A has ΔH = +50 kJ. Same size, opposite sign: whatever heat one direction releases, the other direction must absorb.

Rule 2

Multiply: multiply ΔH

ΔH is for the equation as written. Doubling every coefficient doubles the amount reacting, so it doubles ΔH. Multiplying by ½ halves it. Multiply every species in the equation, not just the one you need.

Rule 3

Add: add ΔH, cancel species

When equations are added, anything that appears on both sides cancels (subtract the smaller coefficient from the larger). Intermediates such as CO₂ in a combustion route must cancel completely to leave the target.

Strategy

Work from the target

Find a species that appears in only one given equation. Its position (left or right) and coefficient in the target tell you whether to reverse that equation and what to multiply by. Then check that everything else cancels.

Phases count

H₂O(l) is not H₂O(g)

Species only cancel if they are identical, phase included. If one equation makes H₂O(g) and another uses H₂O(l), you need a phase-change step (H₂O(l) → H₂O(g), +44 kJ) to connect them, as in the diborane puzzle.

4. Hess's law on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Rewrite each given equation the way you use it (reversed, multiplied) with its new ΔH beside it, then add.
  • Show the sum: ΔH = (+283.0) + (−393.5) = −110.5 kJ, with signs in parentheses.
  • Check the cancellation: every species not in the target must cancel, and every target species must end up on the correct side with the right coefficient.
  • When asked to justify, cite the rules: reversing changes the sign; multiplying by c multiplies ΔH by c; adding equations adds ΔH values.
  • When asked why it works, say total energy is conserved, so the enthalpy change for the overall process equals the sum of the enthalpy changes for the steps.
  • Keep units (kJ or kJ/molrxn) and match the precision of the data.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing an equation but forgetting to change the sign of its ΔH.
  • Multiplying only the species you care about, or forgetting to multiply ΔH.
  • Letting species cancel when their phases differ, e.g. H₂O(l) with H₂O(g).
  • Dropping a given equation you actually need (every species must be accounted for).
  • Adding the original ΔH values instead of the manipulated ones.
  • Expecting every step to be exothermic. Individual steps can have either sign; only the sum matters.
