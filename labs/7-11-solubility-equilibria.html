Introduction to Solubility Equilibria Study Lab

An “insoluble” salt still dissolves a little, and in a saturated solution the dissolving and the precipitating happen at the same rate. The equilibrium constant for that process is Ksp, the solubility product. From Ksp you can calculate how many moles of the salt dissolve per liter, and the reverse. Here you do both for thirteen salts, compare them fairly, and test whether mixing two solutions forms a precipitate.

2. Key ideas

The equilibrium

Dissolving is reversible

For a saturated solution of PbI2, PbI2(s) ⇌ Pb2+(aq) + 2I−(aq). The solid stays in contact with the solution, ions leave and rejoin the crystal at equal rates, and the ion concentrations stay constant.

The expression

Ksp has no denominator

The solid is a pure solid, so it is left out: Ksp = [Pb2+][I−]2. Each ion concentration is raised to its coefficient in the dissolution equation.

Molar solubility

Let s be moles dissolved per liter

If s mol/L of PbI2 dissolves, [Pb2+] = s and [I−] = 2s. Then Ksp = (s)(2s)2 = 4s3. The multiplier and the power both come from the formula.

Going backward

From a measured solubility to Ksp

Convert grams per liter to mol/L with the molar mass, multiply by the subscripts to get each ion concentration, and substitute into the Ksp expression. A measured ion concentration works too: [F−] = 2s for CaF2.

Comparing

Compare Ksp only for the same ion ratio

AgCl (Ksp = 1.8 × 10−10) is less soluble than Ag2CrO4 (Ksp = 1.1 × 10−12), even though its Ksp is larger, because the expressions have different forms. To compare salts with different ratios, calculate s for each.

Solubility rules

Ksp > 1 means soluble

The solubility rules connect to Ksp: salts that are soluble, such as all sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts, have Ksp values greater than 1. The “insoluble” salts of this lab have Ksp values far below 1.

4. Solubility equilibria on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the dissolution equation with (s) and (aq) labels before the Ksp expression.
  • Write the expression without the solid: Ksp = [Ca2+][F−]2.
  • Define s and write each ion concentration in terms of it, then substitute: Ksp = (s)(2s)2 = 4s3.
  • Give units for molar solubility (mol/L, or M) and convert to g/L with the molar mass if asked.
  • When ranking salts of different formula types, calculate s for each; say why comparing Ksp directly fails.
  • For mixing problems, find each ion’s concentration after dilution before calculating Q.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the coefficient inside the parentheses: writing s · s2 instead of s · (2s)2.
  • Reporting [I−] = 2s as the molar solubility, or s as [I−].
  • Putting the solid in the denominator of Ksp.
  • Ranking solubility by Ksp alone across different ion ratios.
  • Using grams per liter in the Ksp expression instead of mol/L.
  • Using the concentrations before mixing to calculate Q when two solutions are combined.
