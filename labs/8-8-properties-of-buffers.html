Properties of Buffers Study Lab

A buffer holds its pH steady because it contains large amounts of both members of a conjugate acid–base pair. The weak acid consumes added OH−; the conjugate base consumes added H3O+. Add strong acid and strong base one particle at a time, watch which particle reacts, and compare the pH with an unbuffered solution.

2. Key ideas

Definition

Both members of a conjugate pair

A buffer contains large concentrations of a weak acid and its conjugate base (CH3COOH and CH3COO−), or of a weak base and its conjugate acid (NH3 and NH4+). One member alone is not a buffer.

Added acid

The base member reacts

Added H3O+ is consumed by the conjugate base: CH3COO− + H3O+ → CH3COOH + H2O. A strong acid is swapped for a weak one, so [H3O+] hardly rises.

Added base

The acid member reacts

Added OH− is consumed by the weak acid: CH3COOH + OH− → CH3COO− + H2O. These reactions go essentially to completion, which is why the buffer can absorb the addition.

Why the pH barely moves

Only the ratio shifts a little

The pH depends on the ratio [A−]/[HA]. When both are large, converting a small amount of one into the other changes that ratio only slightly, so the pH changes only slightly.

Making one

Three recipes

Mix a weak acid with a salt of its conjugate base; or partially neutralize a weak acid with strong base (for example 2 mol CH3COOH + 1 mol NaOH); or partially neutralize a weak base with strong acid. Strong acid plus its salt (HCl + NaCl) is never a buffer.

Limits

A buffer can be used up

Once the base member is gone, more H3O+ has nothing to react with and the pH falls sharply (and likewise for OH−). How much a buffer can absorb is its capacity, topic 8.10. Diluting a buffer keeps the ratio and so keeps the pH, but lowers the capacity.

4. Buffers on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Identify both members of the conjugate pair by formula and say both are present in significant amounts.
  • Write the net ionic equation for the added species: "CH3COO− + H3O+ → CH3COOH + H2O." Use a single arrow; the reaction with a strong acid or base goes to completion.
  • Explain the result: the added H3O+ is consumed, so its concentration does not build up, and [A−]/[HA] changes only slightly.
  • For a particle diagram, convert one particle per added H3O+ or OH−, and keep the total of HA + A− constant.
  • To make a buffer from a weak acid and NaOH, show that the weak acid is in excess: some HA must remain.
  • When asked about a non-buffer, name the missing member of the pair.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the pH "does not change at all." It changes slightly; the change is much smaller than without the buffer.
  • Having the weak acid react with added acid. Added H3O+ reacts with the base member.
  • Calling HCl + NaCl a buffer. Cl− does not react with H3O+.
  • Thinking any weak acid plus NaOH is a buffer. If the moles of NaOH equal or exceed the moles of weak acid, no HA remains.
  • Including Na+ or Cl− in the net ionic equation.
  • Thinking a buffer is always at pH 7. An acetate buffer sits near pH 4.7; an ammonia buffer near 9.3.
