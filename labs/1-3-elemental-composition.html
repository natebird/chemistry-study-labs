2. Key ideas

Fixed ratio

Law of definite proportions

A pure compound always has the same elements in the same proportion by mass. Pure water is always 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass, whether it is a drop or a lake. This is what makes a compound a pure substance.

mass of element ÷ mass of compound

Percent composition

Percent by mass of an element = (mass of that element in one mole ÷ molar mass) × 100. For H₂O: 2(1.008) ÷ 18.02 × 100 = 11.2% H. The percents of all the elements add to 100.

Simplest whole-number ratio

Empirical formula

The empirical formula gives the lowest whole-number ratio of atoms. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, has the empirical formula CH₂O. Ionic compounds are always written as empirical formulas (NaCl, Fe₂O₃).

Percent → grams → moles

From data to formula

Assume a 100 g sample so percents become grams. Convert each mass to moles. Divide every mole value by the smallest. If a ratio ends in about .5, .33, or .25, multiply all of them by 2, 3, or 4.

n = M ÷ empirical mass

Molecular formula

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Divide the compound's molar mass by the empirical formula mass to get that multiple, then multiply every subscript by it.

Same ratio, different compounds

What composition cannot tell you

Compounds with the same empirical formula have the same percent composition. C₂H₂ and C₆H₆ are both 92.3% C. To tell them apart you need more information, such as the molar mass.

4. Composition on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Show each mass-to-moles conversion with its molar mass, e.g. 40.0 g C × (1 mol / 12.01 g) = 3.33 mol C.
  • Show the division by the smallest number of moles and the resulting ratio before writing the formula.
  • When a ratio is near 1.5 or 1.33, state the multiplier you used ("multiply by 2 to get whole numbers").
  • For mass-change data (a metal heated in air, a hydrate heated), find the mass of the second element by subtraction and say so.
  • For the molecular formula, show molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass = a whole number.
  • To justify that a sample is a pure compound, compare its measured percent composition with the value calculated from the formula.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the percents by each other instead of converting to moles first. Formulas are mole ratios, not mass ratios.
  • Rounding 1.5 to 2 (or 1.33 to 1). Only round ratios that are within about 0.1 of a whole number.
  • Rounding moles too early. Keep three or four significant figures until the ratio step.
  • Using the mass of the product (the oxide) as the mass of oxygen. Subtract the metal's mass first.
  • Using the atomic number instead of the molar mass to convert grams to moles.
  • Reducing an ionic formula that is already empirical, or reducing a molecular formula when the question asks for the molecular formula.
