2. Key ideas

K ≫ 1

Essentially to completion

A very large K means the numerator (products) is much larger than the denominator (reactants) at equilibrium. The reaction proceeds until almost all of the limiting reactant is gone. Neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base, K = 1.0 × 1014, is an example.

K ≪ 1

Barely proceeds

A very small K means only a tiny amount of product forms before equilibrium is reached. Water's autoionization (Kw = 1.0 × 10−14) and dissolving AgCl (K = 1.8 × 10−10) are examples: almost everything stays as reactant.

K ≈ 1

Significant amounts of both

When K is near 1, neither side dominates and the equilibrium mixture has substantial amounts of reactants and products. To find the exact amounts you need the reaction's coefficients and starting amounts.

Not speed

K says how far, not how fast

K describes the mixture once equilibrium is reached. It says nothing about how long that takes. A reaction with a huge K can be extremely slow if its activation energy is high.

Log thinking

Count powers of ten

Equilibrium constants span many orders of magnitude, so compare exponents. A K of 10−5 is a thousand times larger than 10−8, and both are "small." Changing K by a factor of 2 hardly changes the picture.

Same reaction, flipped

Reversing flips the verdict

If a reaction has a very large K, its reverse has a very small K (the reciprocal). Neutralization (1014) and autoionization (10−14) are the same equilibrium written in opposite directions. Topic 7.6 covers this.

4. The magnitude of K on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Make a claim and justify it with K: "K ≫ 1, so at equilibrium the concentration of products is much greater than that of reactants."
  • Tie it to the expression: a large K means a large numerator compared with the denominator.
  • For a very small K, say the reaction "proceeds only to a very small extent" and that the reactant concentrations barely change from their initial values.
  • For a very large K, say the reaction "goes essentially to completion," so you can treat the limiting reactant as used up.
  • Compare two reactions by their K values when asked which has the larger extent of reaction (for the same type of reaction).
  • Keep speed out of it unless kinetics data are given: K is about extent, not rate.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a large K means a fast reaction.
  • Saying a small K means "no products." Some product always forms; the amount is just very small.
  • Saying K = 1 means equal concentrations of every species. That is exactly true only for simple 1 : 1 reactions like A ⇌ B.
  • Thinking a K of 10−5 is "close to zero, so the reaction does not occur." It occurs, to a small extent.
  • Reading the sign of the exponent backward: 10−12 is very small, 1012 is very large.
  • Thinking a catalyst changes K. It speeds up both directions equally and leaves the equilibrium mixture unchanged.
