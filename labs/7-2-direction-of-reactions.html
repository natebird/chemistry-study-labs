Direction of Reversible Reactions Study Lab

A reversible reaction always runs both ways at once. Which way it goes, overall, depends only on which of the two rates is larger: a faster forward reaction gives a net conversion of reactants to products, and a faster reverse reaction gives the opposite. Set up a mixture of H2, I2, and HI, race the two rates against each other, then practice calling the direction from numbers and graphs.

2. Key ideas

The rule

The faster direction wins

If the forward rate is greater than the reverse rate, there is a net conversion of reactants to products. If the reverse rate is greater, there is a net conversion of products to reactants. If the rates are equal, the system is at equilibrium.

Both, always

"Net" is the key word

Even while the reaction goes forward overall, some HI molecules are breaking back into H2 and I2. The net direction is the difference between two processes that both occur.

Self-correcting

Rates move toward each other

The faster direction uses up the species that drive it and builds up the species that drive the other direction. So the larger rate falls and the smaller rate rises until they are equal. A reaction never overshoots equilibrium.

Either side

Starting with products

With only HI present, the forward rate is zero because there is no H2 or I2 to collide. The reverse reaction runs first and the forward reaction starts as soon as H2 and I2 form.

Graphs

Reading direction from a graph

On a rate–time graph, the higher curve tells you the direction. On a concentration–time graph, a rising product curve and falling reactant curves mean net forward; flat curves mean equilibrium.

Coming next

The same answer from Q and K

Topic 7.3 introduces the reaction quotient Q. Comparing Q with K predicts the same direction as comparing the rates: Q < K means the forward rate is larger, Q > K means the reverse rate is larger.

4. Direction on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Compare the two rates explicitly: "The rate of the forward reaction is greater than the rate of the reverse reaction."
  • Then state the consequence: "so there is a net conversion of reactants to products."
  • Explain why the rates change: as reactants are consumed the forward rate decreases; as products form the reverse rate increases.
  • End with the equilibrium condition: the change continues until the two rates are equal.
  • When reading a graph, quote the evidence: which curve is higher, or which concentrations are rising or falling, at the time asked about.
  • Use "net" in your answer. It shows you know both reactions are occurring.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "only the forward reaction occurs" before equilibrium. Once any product exists, the reverse reaction occurs too.
  • Deciding direction from which side has more molecules. Direction depends on the rates (or on Q compared with K), not on which concentrations are larger.
  • Thinking the forward rate rises as products form. It depends on reactant amounts, so it falls.
  • Saying the rates become zero at equilibrium. They become equal, and stay nonzero.
  • Reading the direction from the slope of a rate curve instead of from which rate is larger.
  • Assuming a reaction that starts with only products cannot go forward. The forward reaction begins as soon as reactants form.
