2. Key ideas

Sign of ΔH

Negative releases, positive absorbs

ΔH is measured from the point of view of the reacting system. ΔH < 0 (exothermic): the system loses heat to the surroundings. ΔH > 0 (endothermic): the system takes heat in from the surroundings. The sign is about direction; the number is about size.

kJ/molrxn

"Per mole" means per mole of reaction

ΔH belongs to the equation exactly as written. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, ΔH = −92.2 kJ means 92.2 kJ is released when 1 mol N₂ and 3 mol H₂ make 2 mol NH₃. So 46.1 kJ is released per mole of NH₃ formed. Always go through the coefficient.

q = n × ΔH

Heat scales with amount

Twice as much reactant gives twice as much heat. Convert grams to moles, divide by that substance's coefficient to get moles of reaction, then multiply by ΔH. The answer keeps ΔH's sign: negative q means heat released.

Bonds and PE

Where the energy comes from

Breaking bonds takes energy and forming bonds releases it. If the bonds in the products are stronger overall, the products sit at lower potential energy than the reactants, and the difference appears as faster-moving particles: a higher temperature.

System vs surroundings

The thermometer reads the surroundings

A thermometer in the water measures the water, not the reaction. In an exothermic reaction heat flows out of the reacting particles, so the water warms. In an endothermic reaction heat flows into the system from the water, so the water cools, until both reach the same temperature.

Constant pressure

Why ΔH equals the heat

Reactions in open beakers run at constant pressure, where the enthalpy change equals the heat transferred. AP problems treat ΔH as "the heat of reaction"; the CED states that the technical difference between enthalpy and internal energy is not assessed.

4. Enthalpy of reaction on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Show the chain: grams → moles (÷ molar mass) → moles of reaction (÷ coefficient) → heat (× ΔH). Label every number with units.
  • State the direction in words as well as with a sign: "48.2 kJ of heat is released" or "q = −48.2 kJ."
  • When asked to explain a temperature change, say which way heat flows (from the reacting system to the water, or the reverse) and connect it to the kinetic energy of the particles.
  • When asked why a reaction is exothermic, compare bonds: the energy released forming the product bonds is greater than the energy absorbed breaking the reactant bonds.
  • For calorimetry data, set qrxn = −qwater and divide by moles of reaction to get ΔH in kJ/molrxn.
  • Report three significant figures (or match the data) and keep the units kJ or kJ/molrxn straight.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the coefficient: using moles of NH₃ directly with ΔH for 2NH₃, which doubles the answer.
  • Saying "the reaction gets hotter" when you mean the surroundings. The thermometer reads the water.
  • Calling a reaction endothermic because "it needs energy to start." Activation energy (Unit 5) is not ΔH.
  • Saying "breaking bonds releases energy." Breaking bonds always requires energy; forming bonds releases it.
  • Dropping the sign of qwater when converting to qrxn, so an exothermic reaction gets a positive ΔH.
  • Mixing J and kJ: q = mcΔT gives joules; ΔH tables are in kilojoules.
