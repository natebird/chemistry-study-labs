Introduction to Equilibrium Study Lab

Many reactions can run both ways. When a reversible reaction happens in a closed container, it does not stop: it reaches a state where the forward and reverse reactions run at the same rate, so nothing observable changes any more. Seal colorless N2O4 and brown NO2 in a flask, run the clock, and watch that dynamic equilibrium form at the particle level, on a concentration graph, and on a rate graph.

2. Key ideas

Two directions

Reversible processes

A reversible process can go forward (reactants → products) and in reverse (products → reactants). The double arrow ⇌ says both happen. Physical changes such as evaporation and condensation are reversible too, as are proton transfers in acid–base reactions and electron transfers in redox reactions.

What you see

No observable change

At equilibrium the color, pressure, and every concentration stay constant. Reactants and products are both present. "Constant" does not mean "equal": in this flask, equilibrium can have much more N2O4 than NO2, or the other way round.

What is happening

Equilibrium is dynamic

Molecules keep reacting at equilibrium. Every second, some N2O4 splits and the same number of NO2 pairs join. Because the forward and reverse rates are equal, the two processes cancel and there is no net change.

Getting there

Rates converge

The forward rate depends on how much reactant is present, and the reverse rate on how much product is present. Starting with only N2O4, the forward rate starts high and falls while the reverse rate starts at zero and rises, until the two meet.

Reading graphs

Where the curves go flat

On a concentration–time graph, equilibrium begins where every curve becomes horizontal, not where the curves cross. On a rate–time graph, it begins where the forward and reverse rate curves meet and stay together.

Closed system

Both ways, same destination

Starting from pure N2O4, from pure NO2 with the same number of atoms, or from a mix of the two, the flask ends up at the same equilibrium mixture at a given temperature. Equilibrium can be approached from either direction.

3. Reversible processes to recognize

The same pattern applies to physical and chemical processes: two opposing processes, equal rates at equilibrium, constant amounts of everything.

Reading a graph for equilibrium

  1. Concentration (or partial pressure) vs. time: equilibrium starts where all the curves level off. Crossing curves only mean the two amounts happen to be equal at that instant.
  2. Rate vs. time: equilibrium starts where the forward and reverse rate curves meet. After that they stay equal, and neither is zero.
  3. Changes before equilibrium follow the coefficients. Here [NO2] always changes twice as fast as [N2O4] (in the opposite direction), because each N2O4 corresponds to 2 NO2.

4. Equilibrium on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Define equilibrium with rates: "the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction."
  • Connect that to the observation: "so the concentrations (or partial pressures, or the color) remain constant."
  • Say that it is dynamic: both reactions continue at the particle level even though no net change is observed.
  • On a graph, point to the time where all concentrations become constant (or where the rate curves meet) as the start of equilibrium.
  • Use the coefficients to explain the shapes: a species with coefficient 2 changes twice as fast as one with coefficient 1.
  • When drawing a concentration–time sketch, keep products starting at zero if none were added, and end every curve flat.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the reaction "stops" at equilibrium. Both reactions continue at equal rates.
  • Saying reactant and product concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, and usually not equal.
  • Marking equilibrium where two concentration curves cross.
  • Saying the forward and reverse rate constants are equal at equilibrium. The rates are equal; the rate constants generally are not.
  • Thinking a reaction that starts with only products cannot reach the same equilibrium. It can approach from either side.
  • Forgetting that equilibrium requires a closed system: if NO2 could escape, the flask would never settle.
