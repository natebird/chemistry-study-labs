2. Key ideas

The one question

How stable is the conjugate base?

Ionization is HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−. Anything that makes A− more stable (lower in energy) pushes this equilibrium to the right, so Ka is larger and pKa is smaller. Every trend below is a reason A− is more or less stable.

Which H?

Acidic protons sit on electronegative atoms

In CH3COOH only the H on oxygen is acidic, so acetic acid is monoprotic. C–H hydrogens are not acidic in water. Structure tells you which protons take part and how many.

Electronegativity

Pulling electron density away

An electronegative atom holds the negative charge of A− well. Across period 2, HF > H2O > NH3 > CH4 as acids. In H–O–X, a more electronegative X (Cl > Br > I) makes the acid stronger.

Inductive effect and more O

Withdrawing through bonds

Electronegative atoms near the acidic group pull electron density through the bonds and stabilize A−. Each Cl on the carbon next to COOH lowers the pKa. Each extra O on the central atom of an oxyacid does the same, so HClO < HClO2 < HClO3 < HClO4.

Resonance

Spreading the charge

A charge shared over several atoms is more stable than one stuck on a single atom. Acetate spreads −1 over two O atoms; ethoxide (from ethanol) cannot. That is why carboxylic acids, a common class of weak acids, are about 1011 times more acidic than alcohols.

Bases

Strong vs. weak bases

Group 1 and 2 hydroxides are strong bases; their conjugate acid, H2O, is very weak. Common weak bases are nitrogen bases with a lone pair (NH3, amines) and carboxylate ions. The conjugate base of a strong acid (Cl−, NO3−, ClO4−) is too weak to act as a base in water.

4. Structure arguments on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the specific structural difference: "HClO4 has three more O atoms bonded to Cl than HClO does."
  • Connect it to the conjugate base: "the extra O atoms withdraw electron density and delocalize the negative charge of ClO4−, stabilizing it."
  • Finish with the equilibrium consequence: "so HClO4 ionizes more completely and has the larger Ka."
  • Identify the acidic proton in a structure (the H on O in –COOH) and use it to explain why a molecule with several H atoms is monoprotic.
  • When you compare two acids, talk about both of them. "HOCl is stronger because Cl is electronegative" earns less than "Cl is more electronegative than Br, so it pulls more electron density from the O–H bond and stabilizes OCl⁻ more than Br stabilizes OBr⁻."
  • For bases, argue through the conjugate acid: the weaker the conjugate acid, the stronger the base.

Common mistakes

  • Saying HF is the strongest hydrohalic acid because F is most electronegative. Down a group, the very strong H–F bond makes HF the weakest.
  • Counting all H atoms as acidic. CH3COOH releases only the O–H proton.
  • Saying more O atoms make an acid stronger because "there are more O atoms to lose H from." The acidic H count does not change; the electron withdrawal does.
  • Saying the Cl atoms in Cl3CCOOH come off as Cl− ions. They stay bonded; they only pull electron density.
  • Mixing up direction: a stronger acid has a larger Ka and a smaller pKa, and a weaker conjugate base.
  • Calling Cl− a weak base that raises pH. It is the conjugate base of a strong acid and has no measurable effect on pH.
