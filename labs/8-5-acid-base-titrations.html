Acid–Base Titrations Study Lab

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added, and its shape tells you almost everything: whether the analyte is strong or weak, where the equivalence point is, what pKa is, and how many protons a polyprotic acid gives up. Drive the burette along the curve, watch the major species change, and see which indicator would mark the end point.

2. Key ideas

Equivalence point

Moles titrant = moles analyte

For a monoprotic acid or base, the equivalence point is reached when the moles of titrant added equal the moles of analyte originally present. That is true for strong and weak analytes alike, so Veq gives the analyte concentration: CaVa = CtVeq.

Half-equivalence

pH = pKa halfway there

At half the equivalence volume, half of the weak acid has become A⁻, so [HA] = [A⁻] and Henderson–Hasselbalch gives pH = pKa. Reading the pH at ½Veq is how you get pKa from a curve. For a weak base, the pH there equals the pKa of its conjugate acid.

Equivalence pH

Set by what is left

Strong acid + strong base: only spectators, pH 7. Weak acid + strong base: A⁻ remains and makes the solution basic (pH > 7). Weak base + strong acid: BH⁺ remains and makes it acidic (pH < 7).

Reading the shape

Strong vs. weak curves

A weak acid starts at a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration, rises quickly at first, then flattens into a buffer region centered on pKa. Its jump at equivalence is shorter. Past equivalence, both curves follow the excess OH⁻ and look the same.

Indicators

Match the color change to the jump

An indicator is itself a weak acid whose two forms have different colors; it changes color over roughly pKa(indicator) ± 1. Choose one whose range sits inside the steep part of the curve, around the equivalence pH. Phenolphthalein (about 8.2–10.0) suits weak acid + strong base.

Polyprotic acids

One jump per proton

A diprotic acid H₂A shows two equivalence points, the second at twice the volume of the first. Before the first, H₂A and HA⁻ dominate (pH = pKa1 at the first half-equivalence point); between them, HA⁻ and A²⁻ (pH = pKa2 at the second). Count the jumps to count the acidic protons.

Scope: calculating the concentration of each species along a polyprotic titration curve is not assessed on the AP Exam; for monoprotic titrations those calculations are in scope, and qualitative reasoning about which species are present in large or small amounts is in scope for polyprotic acids. That is why the species chart shows numbers only for monoprotic analytes.

4. Titration curves on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Find Veq from the middle of the steep jump, then use moles: mol analyte = Ctitrant × Veq (monoprotic, 1 : 1).
  • Read pKa at ½Veq and say why: "at the half-equivalence point [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pKa." Then Ka = 10−pKa.
  • Explain the equivalence pH with a species and an equation: "CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻ makes the solution basic."
  • For a point before equivalence, show the moles of HA and A⁻ after reaction, then use Henderson–Hasselbalch.
  • For an indicator, compare its pKa (or range) to the pH at the equivalence point.
  • For a polyprotic curve, identify the major species in each region and match each half-equivalence point to its pKa.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the equivalence point is always at pH 7. Only strong + strong titrations are neutral there.
  • Reading pKa at the equivalence point instead of halfway to it.
  • Thinking a weak acid needs less base to reach equivalence. Strength changes the curve's shape, not the moles of base needed.
  • Thinking that adding water to the flask changes Veq. It changes the starting pH a little but not the moles of analyte.
  • Picking methyl red or methyl orange for a weak acid + strong base titration; they change color well before equivalence.
  • Treating the end point and the equivalence point as the same thing: the end point is where the indicator changes, which a good indicator places close to equivalence.
