Galvanic (Voltaic) and Electrolytic Cells Study Lab

An electrochemical cell splits a redox reaction into two half-cells, so the electrons have to travel through a wire. In a galvanic cell the reaction is thermodynamically favored and produces a current; in an electrolytic cell a power supply forces an unfavored reaction to run. Build cells from two half-cells, run them, and track every moving part.

2. Key ideas

Always true

Oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode

This holds for every electrochemical cell, galvanic or electrolytic. What changes between the two types is which half-cell gets to be the anode. Memory aid: An Ox and a Red Cat.

Thermodynamics

Galvanic vs. electrolytic

A galvanic (voltaic) cell runs a thermodynamically favored reaction (E°cell > 0, ΔG° < 0) and pushes current through a load. An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to drive a thermodynamically unfavored reaction (the reverse direction).

Two paths

Electrons in the wire, ions in the bridge

Electrons travel only through the external wire, from anode to cathode. They never cross the salt bridge or the solutions. Inside the cell, charge is carried by ions moving through the solutions and the salt bridge.

Salt bridge

Keeps each beaker neutral

The anode beaker gains cations as metal dissolves; the cathode beaker loses them as they plate out. Anions from the bridge flow toward the anode and cations toward the cathode, so neither beaker builds up charge. Without it, the current stops almost at once.

Macroscopic clues

What you can see

The anode metal dissolves and loses mass; metal plates onto the cathode and it gains mass. A colored ion (blue Cu2+) fades where it is reduced. A gas such as H2 bubbles off where H+ is reduced. Each clue tells you which half-reaction happens where.

The meter

Voltmeter or power supply

In a galvanic cell the external device measures the voltage (or uses the current). In an electrolytic cell it is a power source that must supply more than the magnitude of E°cell to push electrons the "wrong" way.

4. Cells on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Identify the anode by the half-reaction that is an oxidation, and the cathode by the reduction. Write each half-reaction with electrons shown.
  • Give the direction of electron flow in the wire as "from the X electrode to the Y electrode."
  • For the salt bridge, say which ions move toward which half-cell and why (to balance the charge being produced or consumed).
  • Connect observations to half-reactions: "the Cu electrode gains mass because Cu2+ is reduced to Cu(s) there."
  • Classify the cell with thermodynamics: favored reaction, E°cell > 0 → galvanic; external source drives an unfavored reaction → electrolytic.
  • For experimental-design questions, trace the change through the cell (removing the bridge stops the current; a bigger electrode does not change the voltage).

Common mistakes

  • Saying electrons flow through the salt bridge or the solution. Only ions move there.
  • Thinking the anode is always the same metal. In an electrolytic cell the roles reverse compared with the galvanic cell made from the same half-cells.
  • Sending K+ toward the anode. Cations go to the cathode beaker, which is losing cations.
  • Saying the cathode loses mass. Reduction deposits metal, so the cathode gains mass (or produces a gas).
  • Spending exam time on which electrode is "+" or "−". Those labels are not assessed; anode/cathode and oxidation/reduction are.
  • Forgetting to balance electrons when combining half-reactions into the overall equation.
