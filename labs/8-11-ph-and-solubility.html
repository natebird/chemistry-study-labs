pH and Solubility Study Lab

A slightly soluble salt dissolves more in acid when one of its ions is a base: H3O+ reacts with that ion, lowering its concentration, and by Le Châtelier's principle more solid dissolves to replace it. If the anion is the conjugate base of a strong acid, the pH has no effect. Move the pH over four classic salts and see which ones respond.

pH-sensitive?

Solubilities are computed for a solution held at the given pH, from Ksp and Ka values at 25 °C (CaF2 3.9 × 10−11, Mg(OH)2 5.6 × 10−12, CaCO3 4.8 × 10−9, AgCl 1.8 × 10−10; HF Ka 6.8 × 10−4; H2CO3 Ka1 4.3 × 10−7, Ka2 4.8 × 10−11). They ignore activity effects, ion pairing, and CO2 escaping, and values above about 1 M only mean "dissolves completely." They are illustrations: the AP exam asks for the qualitative effect of pH on solubility, and computing solubility as a function of pH is not assessed.

2. Key ideas

When pH matters

One ion is a base (or OH−)

Solubility depends on pH when an ion of the salt is a weak base (F−, CO32−, CH3COO−, PO43−), the hydroxide ion itself, or a weak acid. The usual examples are basic anions.

Le Châtelier

Removing a product pulls more solid in

In CaF2(s) ⇌ Ca2+ + 2F−, added H3O+ converts F− into HF. Lower [F−] makes Q < Ksp, so more CaF2 dissolves until Q = Ksp again.

Hydroxides

Acid dissolves them; base suppresses them

H3O+ + OH− → 2H2O removes hydroxide, so Mg(OH)2 dissolves in stomach acid. Added OH− is a common ion and pushes the equilibrium left, lowering solubility.

Carbonates

Fizz in acid

CO32− picks up protons to become HCO3− and then H2CO3, which breaks down into CO2 and water. Limestone and marble dissolve in acid rain for this reason.

No effect

Anions of strong acids

Cl−, Br−, I−, NO3− and ClO4− do not react with H3O+, so the solubility of AgCl or AgBr is the same in acid as in water.

Ksp vs. solubility

Ksp stays put

At constant temperature, Ksp does not change with pH. What changes is the molar solubility: how much solid must dissolve to make Q equal Ksp again.

4. pH and solubility on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the dissolution equilibrium first: CaF2(s) ⇌ Ca2+(aq) + 2F−(aq).
  • Write the reaction of the added species with the ion: F− + H3O+ → HF + H2O.
  • State the chain: [F−] decreases, so Q < Ksp (or the equilibrium shifts right by Le Châtelier's principle), so more solid dissolves.
  • Explain why the anion reacts: it is the conjugate base of a weak acid (HF).
  • For a "no effect" salt, say why: Cl− is the conjugate base of a strong acid and does not react with H3O+.
  • Keep it qualitative: the course asks for the direction of the effect, not a calculated solubility.

Common mistakes

  • Saying Ksp increases in acid. Ksp depends only on temperature; the solubility increases.
  • Saying every salt dissolves better in acid. AgCl does not.
  • Forgetting the common-ion effect for hydroxides: adding NaOH lowers the solubility of Mg(OH)2.
  • Having H3O+ react with the metal cation. It reacts with the basic anion.
  • Writing a double arrow for H3O+ + OH− → 2H2O as if it barely happens. It goes essentially to completion.
  • Saying the solid dissolves "because acid is corrosive." Use the equilibrium argument.
