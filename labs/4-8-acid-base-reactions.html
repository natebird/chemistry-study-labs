In the Brønsted–Lowry model, an acid is a proton (H+) donor and a base is a proton acceptor, so every acid–base reaction is a proton transfer. Removing an H+ from an acid gives its conjugate base; adding an H+ to a base gives its conjugate acid. Choose an acid and a base, watch the proton move, and name the conjugate pairs.

2. Key ideas

Definitions

Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases

An acid donates a proton (H+); a base accepts one. A base needs a lone pair of electrons to form the new bond to H+. The acid and base are defined by what they do in a particular reaction, not by a label on the bottle.

One H⁺ apart

Conjugate acid–base pairs

Take one H+ off an acid and you have its conjugate base (charge goes down by 1). Put one H+ on a base and you have its conjugate acid (charge goes up by 1). Every acid–base reaction has two pairs.

Amphoteric

Water can be either

Water accepts a proton from HCl (acting as a base, becoming H3O+) and donates one to NH3 (acting as an acid, becoming OH−). Species that can do both, like H2O, HCO3−, and H2PO4−, are amphoteric.

Autoionization

Water reacts with itself

In pure water a tiny fraction of molecules transfer a proton to each other: H2O + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + OH−. One water molecule is the acid and the other is the base. Unit 8 puts a number on it (Kw).

Water's role

Why water matters in solution

When an acid or a base dissolves in water, water is usually the partner in the proton transfer. A strong acid such as HCl gives up its proton to water completely, so the solution contains H3O+ and Cl−, not HCl molecules.

Reversible

Both directions are acid–base

In the reverse reaction, the conjugate acid donates the proton back to the conjugate base. That is why a double arrow (⇌) is used for weak acids and bases. Which direction is favored is a Unit 8 question.

4. Acid–base on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Identify the acid as "the proton (H+) donor" and the base as "the proton acceptor" in that specific reaction.
  • Write conjugate pairs as acid/base pairs that differ by one H+: NH4+/NH3, H2O/OH−.
  • Check charges when writing a conjugate: removing H+ lowers the charge by 1; adding H+ raises it by 1.
  • When asked how water behaves, say what it does in that reaction: "H2O acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid because it donates a proton to NH3."
  • For a strong acid in water, represent the solution as H3O+ and the anion, with no intact acid molecules.
  • Balance both atoms and charge in every proton-transfer equation.

Common mistakes

  • Pairing the two reactants (or the two products) as a conjugate pair. A pair has one species on each side.
  • Writing the conjugate base of H2PO4− as PO43−. Only one H+ comes off: HPO42−.
  • Calling OH− the acid in NH4+ + OH− because it "has an H." The acid is the species that loses H+.
  • Thinking every H in a formula is acidic. In CH3COOH only the H bonded to O is donated.
  • Saying water is "neutral, so it can't react." Water is amphoteric and takes part in most acid–base reactions in solution.
  • Mixing up the Brønsted–Lowry model with "acids contain H, bases contain OH." NH3 and CO32− are bases with no OH.
