2. Key ideas

Homogeneous

A solution looks the same everywhere

In a solution the solute is spread out as individual particles (ions or molecules) among the solvent particles. Every part of the sample has the same composition and properties. Solutions can be liquid (salt water), solid (brass) or gas (air).

Heterogeneous

A mixture whose properties depend on where you look

In a heterogeneous mixture you can find regions with different composition: sand in water, oil on vinegar, a granite rock. A sample taken from one spot is not like a sample from another.

M = n / V

Molarity

Molarity is moles of solute per liter of solution, not per liter of solvent. That is why you dissolve the solid in some water first and then add water up to the mark: the final volume of the whole solution is what counts.

Ions

Count the ions from the formula

A soluble ionic compound separates into its ions. 0.10 M CaCl2 has [Ca2+] = 0.10 M and [Cl−] = 0.20 M. Polyatomic ions such as SO42− stay together. A molecular solute such as glucose stays as whole molecules.

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

Dilution keeps the moles

Adding solvent spreads the same moles of solute through a larger volume. The moles you pipet out (M₁V₁) are the moles in the new flask (M₂V₂). Concentration drops by the same factor the volume grows.

Glassware

Precision matters

A volumetric flask and a volumetric pipet are made to hold or deliver one exact volume (to about ±0.1%). A beaker is good only to about ±5–10%. For a solution of known concentration, the AP answer is an analytical balance plus a volumetric flask.

4. Answering it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Show the moles step: write n = m ÷ molar mass or n = M × V with units, then divide by the volume in liters.
  • Describe the procedure in order: weigh the solid on an analytical balance, transfer it to a volumetric flask, dissolve it in some distilled water, add water to the calibration mark, then stopper and invert to mix.
  • For a dilution, name a volumetric pipet (or buret) for the measured stock and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
  • For ion concentrations, multiply by the subscript and say why: each formula unit releases that many ions.
  • Report the answer with units (M or mol/L) and sensible significant figures, usually three.
  • When asked about a particle diagram, keep the ratio of ions correct and the number of solute particles proportional to concentration.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by milliliters. 0.0250 mol in 250.0 mL is 0.100 M, not 1.00 × 10−4 M.
  • Adding the solid to 250.0 mL of water. The solid takes up space, so the solution is no longer exactly 250.0 mL. Fill to the mark instead.
  • Thinking dilution changes the moles of solute. Only the volume changes.
  • Forgetting the subscript: [Cl−] in 0.10 M MgCl2 is 0.20 M.
  • Breaking up polyatomic ions or dissociating a molecular solute like glucose or ethanol.
  • Using the anhydrous molar mass when you weigh a hydrate.
