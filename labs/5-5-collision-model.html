Collision Model Study Lab

For an elementary reaction to happen, reactant particles must collide, and most collisions do nothing. A collision succeeds only if the particles hit hard enough to overcome the activation energy and are lined up so the right bonds can break and form. Here you watch both requirements play out in a particle simulator and connect them to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of energies.

2. Key ideas

Requirement 1

They must collide

Bonds can only break and form when reactant particles actually hit each other. So anything that makes collisions more frequent, such as a higher concentration, speeds up the reaction.

Requirement 2

Enough energy

A collision must supply at least the activation energy, Ea, to start breaking bonds. Slow, gentle collisions just bounce. In most reactions only a small fraction of collisions have enough energy.

Requirement 3

The right orientation

The particles must meet so that the atoms that need to bond are next to each other. In AB + C → A + BC, C has to strike the B end; a hit on the A end does nothing, however hard.

Energy spread

Maxwell–Boltzmann curve

Particles in a sample have a wide range of kinetic energies. The area under the curve to the right of Ea estimates the fraction of collisions energetic enough to react. The total area stays the same: it represents all the particles.

Temperature

Why heating works so well

At a higher temperature the curve flattens and shifts right, so the area past Ea grows a lot. Collisions also get a little more frequent, but the larger fraction of energetic collisions is the main reason the rate rises.

What does not change

Concentration vs. fraction

More particles in the same volume collide more often, but the fraction of collisions with enough energy depends only on temperature and Ea. A catalyst works the other way: it lowers Ea (topic 5.11).

4. The collision model on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • State both conditions for a successful collision: energy at least Ea and an orientation that lets the bonds rearrange.
  • For temperature, say "a greater fraction of collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy." That phrase is the key point.
  • For concentration, say "more particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions," and that the fraction of successful collisions is unchanged.
  • When drawing a second Maxwell–Boltzmann curve for a higher temperature, make it lower and broader with its peak shifted to higher energy, and keep the area about the same.
  • Point to the area to the right of Ea when explaining with the graph.
  • Connect particle-level reasoning to the observed rate: more successful collisions per second means a faster rate.

Common mistakes

  • Saying higher temperature lowers the activation energy. Ea stays the same; more particles reach it.
  • Giving only "more collisions" as the reason heating speeds a reaction.
  • Saying every collision with enough energy reacts. Orientation matters too.
  • Drawing the higher-temperature curve taller, or with a larger total area.
  • Saying higher concentration gives particles more energy.
  • Confusing "collision frequency" (how often) with "fraction of effective collisions" (how many of them work).
