Reaction Quotient and Le Châtelier’s Principle Study Lab

Le Châtelier’s principle predicts the direction of a shift; Q and K explain it. A disturbance makes Q differ from K, and the system reacts until Q equals K again. Changing a concentration, the volume, or diluting a solution changes Q only. Changing the temperature changes K itself. Here you apply a stress, calculate Q at the instant after it, and follow the system to its new equilibrium.

2. Key ideas

The mechanism

Stress → Q ≠ K → shift

Before the stress, Q = K. The stress changes Q (or K), so they no longer match. If Q < K the net reaction runs forward; if Q > K it runs in reverse. It stops shifting when Q = K again.

Concentration

Q moves, K stays

Adding a product or removing a reactant makes the numerator bigger or the denominator smaller, so Q > K and the reaction shifts left. Adding a reactant or removing a product makes Q < K: shift right.

Volume

Every gas changes by the same factor

Halving the volume doubles every gas concentration. Q is multiplied by 2Δn, where Δn = moles of gas on the right − moles on the left. If Δn = 0, Q does not change and there is no shift.

Dilution

The same idea in solution

Doubling the volume of a solution halves every dissolved concentration, so Q is multiplied by (½)Δn with Δn counted over dissolved species. For Fe3+ + SCN− ⇌ FeSCN2+, Δn = −1, so Q doubles and the system shifts left.

Temperature

K moves, Q stays

At the instant the temperature changes, no concentration has changed, so Q is still the old K. The new K is larger for an endothermic reaction that is heated and smaller for an exothermic one, and the system shifts to reach it.

After the shift

Q equals K again

At the new equilibrium Q equals K: the same K as before if the temperature did not change. The individual concentrations are different, but they combine in the expression to the same value.

4. Q, K and shifts on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write Q with the concentrations right after the change, and compare it with K in words: "Q is now less than K."
  • Connect the comparison to the direction: "so the forward reaction rate exceeds the reverse rate until Q = K."
  • For volume changes, multiply every gas concentration by the same factor before calculating Q; don't just count moles.
  • For temperature changes, say explicitly that K changes and which way, using the sign of ΔH.
  • When asked for the new equilibrium amounts, set up an ICE table that starts from the disturbed amounts, not the original ones.
  • Note that a new equilibrium at the same temperature has the same value of K.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a concentration change "changes K." It changes Q.
  • Saying a temperature change "changes Q." At that instant only K changes.
  • Forgetting the exponents: halving [NH3] divides Q by 4, not 2.
  • Starting the ICE table for the new equilibrium from the old equilibrium amounts instead of the amounts right after the stress.
  • Thinking a reaction with equal moles of gas on both sides shifts when compressed.
  • Expecting the added species to return all the way to its old concentration.
