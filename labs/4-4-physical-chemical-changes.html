Physical and Chemical Changes Study Lab

The difference between a physical and a chemical change lives at the particle level. If chemical bonds break and new ones form, making new substances, the change is chemical. If only the attractions between particles change, as in melting or boiling, it is physical. Dissolving a salt sits on the line, and the CED says you can argue it either way. 2. Key ideas

Bonds break and form

Chemical processes

Processes that break and/or form chemical bonds (covalent, ionic, or metallic) are typically classified as chemical. Atoms end up bonded to different partners, so the products are new substances with different properties.

Only IMFs change

Physical processes

Processes that only change the intermolecular interactions between particles, such as phase changes, are typically physical. The molecules in steam are the same H₂O molecules as in ice; only how close and how ordered they are has changed.

The gray zone

Dissolving a salt

Dissolving NaCl breaks the ionic bonds of the crystal lattice and forms new ion–dipole attractions with water. No new substance with a new composition appears: the same Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are present. The CED says plausible arguments can be made for either classification.

Molecular solutes

Dissolving sugar is physical

When sucrose dissolves, whole sucrose molecules separate from each other and form hydrogen bonds with water. No covalent bonds inside the molecules break. Compare HCl, which reacts with water: its H–Cl bond breaks and H₃O⁺ forms, so dissolving HCl is chemical.

Macroscopic link

Properties follow the particles

New substances bring new properties: a new color, a new state at room temperature, a new odor or reactivity. A physical change keeps the substance's identity, so it can often be reversed by a physical method like cooling or evaporating.

Energy is not the test

Both kinds involve energy

Breaking attractions always takes energy and forming them always releases energy, whether the attractions are bonds or IMFs. Phase changes and reactions both absorb or release heat. Classify by what breaks and forms, not by whether there is a temperature change.

4. Answering the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the interaction that changes, precisely: "hydrogen bonds between water molecules," "the covalent O–H bonds within water molecules," "ionic bonds in the lattice," "ion–dipole interactions."
  • Distinguish between molecules (intermolecular forces) from within molecules (covalent bonds). Graders read those words closely.
  • For a chemical change, name at least one bond broken and one bond formed, or name the new substance.
  • For a physical change, say the particles keep their identity: "the H₂O molecules are unchanged; only the distance between them increases."
  • For dissolving an ionic compound, either answer can earn credit, but it must be backed with a particle-level reason.
  • Tie particle reasoning to observations when asked: a new color, gas, or solid supports the formation of a new substance.

Common mistakes

  • Saying boiling water breaks the bonds in water and makes hydrogen and oxygen. Boiling separates whole molecules.
  • Calling a hydrogen bond a chemical bond in this context. Hydrogen bonds are intermolecular forces.
  • Classifying by energy: "it got cold, so it is chemical." Physical changes absorb and release energy too.
  • Treating all dissolving the same. Sugar dissolving is physical; HCl dissolving in water is a chemical reaction.
  • Saying a change is physical because it is reversible. Many chemical reactions can be reversed, and some physical changes are hard to undo.
  • Answering "it depends" on dissolving a salt without choosing and justifying a side.
