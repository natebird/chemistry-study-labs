Cell Potential Under Nonstandard Conditions Study Lab

E° describes a cell at standard conditions, where Q = 1. Real cells rarely sit there. The cell potential is a measure of how far the reaction is from equilibrium: push Q away from K and |E| grows, let Q approach K and |E| shrinks to zero. Change the concentrations, read Q, and predict the voltage before you look.

2. Key ideas

The anchor

E° means Q = 1

Standard conditions (1 M solutions, 1 atm gases) make Q = 1, and the cell potential is E°. Any other concentrations give a different Q and a different E.

The rule

Q < 1 raises E; Q > 1 lowers E

From E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q: if Q < 1, ln Q < 0 and E > E°. If Q > 1, E < E°. More reactant ions or fewer product ions push Q down and the voltage up.

Driving force

E measures distance from equilibrium

The farther Q is from K, the larger |E|. As a galvanic cell runs, products build up, Q rises toward K, and the voltage falls. At Q = K the cell is at equilibrium and E = 0: the battery is dead.

Not Le Châtelier

Reason with Q, not "shifts"

A working cell is not at equilibrium, so the CED says equilibrium arguments such as Le Châtelier's principle do not apply. Explain voltage changes by how a change moves Q relative to 1 (for E vs. E°) or relative to K.

Concentration cells

Same half-cell, different concentrations

With the same metal on both sides, E° = 0. Electrons flow in whatever direction moves the concentrations toward each other: the dilute side is oxidized (its ion concentration rises) and the concentrated side is reduced. E = 0 when the concentrations are equal.

What matters in Q

Only aqueous ions and gases

Solid electrodes are left out of Q. Coefficients become exponents, so for Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag, Q = [Cu2+]/[Ag+]2, and diluting Ag+ tenfold raises Q a hundredfold.

4. Nonstandard cells on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write Q for the overall reaction first (aqueous species only, coefficients as exponents).
  • Say how the change moves Q relative to 1: "Lowering [Cu2+] increases Q above 1."
  • Connect Q to E with the Nernst equation qualitatively: "Since Q > 1, ln Q > 0, so E < E°."
  • Or argue with equilibrium distance: "Q is now closer to K, so the driving force, and |E|, is smaller."
  • For concentration cells, identify the anode as the dilute side and give electron flow toward the concentrated side.
  • For a dead battery, state that Q = K and E = 0.

Common mistakes

  • Using Le Châtelier's principle ("the reaction shifts right") as the justification. Reason with Q instead.
  • Including solid metals in Q, or forgetting the exponent from a coefficient.
  • Thinking E° changes with concentration. E° is fixed; E is what changes.
  • Saying a concentration cell produces no voltage because E° = 0. It does whenever the concentrations differ.
  • Thinking a bigger electrode raises the voltage. Solids do not appear in Q.
  • Getting the sign backward: Q > 1 lowers E, Q < 1 raises it.
