Oxidation–Reduction (Redox) Reactions Study Lab

In a redox reaction, electrons move from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced. Oxidation numbers are the bookkeeping tool that shows where they went, and half-reactions are the tool that balances the equation. Use Part A to assign oxidation numbers, then Part B to balance real redox reactions in acidic solution one step at a time.

1. Oxidation numbers and half-reactions

An oxidation number is a hypothetical charge: the charge an atom would have if every bond were fully ionic, with shared electrons given to the more electronegative atom. Every equation on this page is checked atom by atom and charge by charge as you go.

    2. Key ideas

    Bookkeeping

    Oxidation numbers

    An oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic. It is not a real charge (Mn in MnO4− is not a 7+ ion), but tracking it shows exactly which atoms gain or lose electrons.

    OIL RIG

    Oxidized and reduced

    Oxidation is loss of electrons, so the oxidation number goes up. Reduction is gain of electrons, so it goes down. They always happen together: the electrons one species loses are the electrons the other gains.

    Agents

    Oxidizing and reducing agents

    The oxidizing agent causes oxidation by taking electrons, so it is the species that is reduced. The reducing agent gives electrons away, so it is the species that is oxidized. Name the whole species (MnO4−), not just the atom. The AP Exam itself does not test these two terms (a CED exclusion), but your class and textbook will use them.

    Half-reactions

    Split, balance, recombine

    Write the oxidation and reduction separately. Balance each for atoms and charge, with electrons as a reactant (reduction) or a product (oxidation). Then multiply so the electrons match and add.

    Acidic solution

    H2O and H+ fill the gaps

    Oxygen-containing ions such as MnO4− lose or gain O atoms. In acidic solution, add H2O to balance O and H+ to balance H. They are really there: the reaction uses up acid or makes water.

    Final check

    Atoms and charge

    A correct redox equation has the same number of each atom and the same total charge on both sides, and no electrons left over. If the charges don't match, the electron count is wrong.

    4. Redox on the FRQ

    What earns points on the FRQ

    • Give oxidation numbers before and after: "Fe is oxidized from +2 to +3." The numbers are the evidence.
    • Write half-reactions with electrons on the correct side: products for oxidation, reactants for reduction.
    • Show the multiplication that makes electrons equal, then add. The final equation has no electrons in it.
    • Check atoms and charge in the final answer, and reduce the coefficients to the lowest whole numbers.
    • When asked for the number of electrons transferred, give the total in the balanced equation (5 for MnO4− + 5Fe2+), not per atom.
    • Name agents as species: "MnO4− is the oxidizing agent," not "Mn is the oxidizing agent."

    Common mistakes

    • Mixing up the agents. The oxidizing agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidized.
    • Counting electrons per atom when the species has two of that atom: Cr2O72− → 2Cr3+ takes 6 electrons, not 3.
    • Balancing O with O2 or O2− in acidic solution. Use H2O, then H+.
    • Adding electrons to the wrong side, which leaves the charges unbalanced. Always count charge after adding e−.
    • Forgetting to cancel H2O and H+ that appear on both sides after adding the halves.
    • Treating the oxidation number as the real charge on the atom. Mn in MnO4− has an oxidation number of +7, but the ion's charge is −1.
