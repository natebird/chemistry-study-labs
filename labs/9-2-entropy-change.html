Absolute Entropy and Entropy Change Study Lab

Unlike enthalpy, entropy has a true zero: a perfect crystal at 0 K. That is why tables list an absolute standard molar entropy, S°, for every substance, elements included. The entropy change of a reaction is the total S° of the products minus the total S° of the reactants, each multiplied by its coefficient. Build that sum below and see how the sign matches the particle picture from topic 9.1.

2. Key ideas

Absolute scale

Entropy has a true zero

A perfect crystal at 0 K has only one possible arrangement, so its entropy is zero. Every real substance at 25 °C has S° > 0, and tables give that absolute value. Enthalpy has no such zero, which is why ΔH°f is defined relative to elements.

Elements

Elements are not zero

ΔH°f and ΔG°f of an element in its standard state are zero by definition. S° is not: O₂(g) has S° = 205.2 J/(mol·K). Leaving elements out of a ΔS° sum is one of the most common errors.

The equation

Products minus reactants

ΔS° = ΣnS°(products) − ΣnS°(reactants), where n is each coefficient. This equation is on the AP equations sheet. Multiply each S° by its coefficient before adding.

Scaling

ΔS° follows the equation

Reversing a reaction changes the sign of ΔS°. Doubling every coefficient doubles ΔS°. The units J/(mol·K) mean "per mole of reaction as written," so ΔS° belongs to the equation exactly as it is balanced.

Trends in S°

What makes S° large

Gases have much larger S° than liquids, and liquids larger than solids. In the same phase, larger and more complex molecules have larger S° (more atoms that can vibrate and rotate): CH₄ 186.3, C₃H₈ 269.9 J/(mol·K).

Sanity check

Does the sign make sense?

After calculating, compare the sign with the moles of gas. Fewer moles of gas should give a negative ΔS°. If your arithmetic disagrees with the particle picture, look for a missing coefficient or an element left out.

4. ΔS° calculations on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Write ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants), then substitute each value multiplied by its coefficient.
  • Include every species, including elements such as O₂(g), H₂(g), and N₂(g).
  • Use the S° value for the correct phase: H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) differ by about 119 J/(mol·K).
  • Report units of J/(molrxn·K), or J/K for the reaction as written, with the correct sign.
  • If asked to justify the sign, connect it to the particle picture: the change in moles of gas or phase.
  • If you need ΔS° in ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°, convert it to kJ/(mol·K) by dividing by 1000.

Common mistakes

  • Setting S° = 0 for elements, as you would for ΔH°f. Only ΔH°f and ΔG°f are zero for elements.
  • Forgetting to multiply by coefficients, especially the 2 in front of H₂O or NH₃.
  • Subtracting in the wrong order (reactants − products), which flips the sign.
  • Mixing up H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) values.
  • Reporting ΔS° in kJ when the table gives J, or mixing the two in one sum.
  • Forgetting that reversing or scaling the equation reverses or scales ΔS°.
