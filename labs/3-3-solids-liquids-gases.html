2. Key ideas

Solid

Fixed positions

Particles are in contact and held in place by attractions. They vibrate but do not move past each other, so a solid keeps its shape and volume. In a crystalline solid the particles form a repeating pattern; in an amorphous solid (glass, many plastics) they do not.

Liquid

Close, but mobile

Particles are still in contact, so a liquid's volume is nearly the same as the solid's. They have enough energy to slide past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. There is no long-range order.

Gas

Far apart and fast

Particles are far apart compared with their size and move freely in straight lines between collisions. A gas fills its container and is easy to compress. A mole of gas takes up roughly a thousand times the volume of a mole of the liquid.

IMF decides the phase

Melting and boiling points

To melt or boil, particles need enough kinetic energy to overcome the attractions holding them together. Stronger intermolecular forces mean higher melting and boiling points. Molecules stay intact; only the forces between them are overcome.

Vapor pressure

Escaping the liquid

In a sealed container, molecules at the surface with enough energy escape into the vapor while others condense back. At equilibrium the two rates are equal, and the pressure of the vapor is the vapor pressure. Weaker IMFs mean higher vapor pressure.

Boiling

When vapor pressure reaches the outside pressure

A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure above it. The normal boiling point is the temperature where the vapor pressure is 760 torr (1 atm). Lower the outside pressure, as on a mountain, and the liquid boils at a lower temperature.

4. Phases on the FRQ

What earns points

  • In a particle diagram of a solid or liquid, draw particles touching; for a gas, draw them far apart. Keep the number of particles the same when a substance changes phase.
  • Draw a crystalline solid as a regular pattern; a liquid as touching particles with no pattern.
  • Draw whole molecules in every phase. Boiling water gives H2O molecules in the gas, not H2 and O2.
  • Rank vapor pressure in the opposite order from IMF strength, and name the IMF: "ether has the highest vapor pressure because it cannot hydrogen bond with itself."
  • Explain vapor pressure with energy: a larger fraction of surface molecules have enough kinetic energy to overcome the attractions.
  • For boiling, compare vapor pressure with the external pressure, not just with a temperature.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing liquid particles spread far apart. Liquids are nearly as dense as solids.
  • Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance melts or boils. Only intermolecular forces are overcome.
  • Saying more liquid or a bigger container raises the vapor pressure. It depends only on the substance and the temperature.
  • Saying a liquid with a high vapor pressure has strong IMFs. It is the reverse.
  • Saying boiling happens at 100 °C for every liquid, or at every pressure. Water boils at about 95 °C where the air pressure is 630 torr.
  • Thinking ice is denser than water. Hydrogen bonds hold ice in an open pattern, so ice floats; for most other substances the solid is denser than the liquid.
