Bond Enthalpies Study Lab

Every reaction breaks bonds, which costs energy, and forms bonds, which releases energy. Adding up average bond enthalpies on both sides gives an estimate of ΔH. Step through the energy staircase below, change bond strengths, and see why the estimate lands close to, but not exactly on, the measured value.

1. The energy staircase

Imagine a reaction in two steps: first break every bond in the reactants to make separated gas-phase atoms (uphill), then form every bond in the products from those atoms (downhill). ΔH is where you end up relative to where you started. Bond values are common textbook averages in kJ/mol; all species are gases.

2. Key ideas

Breaking costs

Bond breaking is always endothermic

Atoms in a bond sit in a potential-energy well (topic 2.2). Pulling them apart means climbing out of that well, so breaking any bond absorbs energy. Forming a bond is the reverse and always releases the same amount.

The equation

ΔH ≈ Σ broken − Σ formed

Add the bond enthalpies of every bond broken in the reactants (energy in), subtract the bond enthalpies of every bond formed in the products (energy out). The order matters: reactant bonds first. This is the reverse of the ΔH°f equation, which is products minus reactants.

Exo or endo

Stronger product bonds mean exothermic

If the bonds formed are stronger overall than the bonds broken, more energy is released than absorbed and ΔH is negative. If the reactant bonds are stronger overall, ΔH is positive. Count the bonds, don't just compare one bond to another.

Count carefully

Coefficients × bonds per molecule

2 H₂O has 4 O–H bonds, not 2. CO₂ has two C=O double bonds. Draw the Lewis structure (topic 2.5) to know which bonds are single, double, or triple; a C=C and a C–C have very different values.

Shortcut

Bonds that survive cancel

In CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl, three C–H bonds are in both the reactants and the products. Counting them on both sides adds and subtracts the same energy, so you get the same ΔH by counting only the bonds that actually change.

Why the answer is approximate

Tabulated values are averages over many molecules; a C–H bond in CH₄ is not exactly as strong as one in CH₃Cl. The method also assumes every species is a gas. ΔH°f data (topic 6.8) give more accurate values.

4. Bond enthalpies on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Write the setup: ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed), then list each bond with its count, e.g. "4(C–H) + 2(O=O)".
  • Draw or picture Lewis structures first so you count double and triple bonds correctly.
  • Multiply bonds per molecule by the coefficient of that molecule.
  • Explain the sign with bonds: "more energy is released forming the C=O and O–H bonds than is absorbed breaking the C–H and O=O bonds, so the reaction is exothermic."
  • To find an unknown bond enthalpy, set up the same equation with ΔH given and solve for the one unknown.
  • If asked why the result differs from a measured value, say bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds (and assume gas-phase species).

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting in the wrong order (formed − broken), which flips the sign.
  • Saying bond breaking releases energy. It never does.
  • Forgetting coefficients: 2 NH₃ has six N–H bonds.
  • Treating CO₂ as two C–O single bonds instead of two C=O double bonds, or O₂ as O–O.
  • Mixing methods: using ΔH°f values in the broken − formed equation, or bond enthalpies in products − reactants.
  • Using the method with liquids (like H₂O(l)) without noting that bond enthalpies describe gas-phase molecules.
