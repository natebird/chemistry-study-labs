Endothermic and Exothermic Processes Study Lab

A thermometer cannot see energy, but it can see where energy went. If the water around a process warms up, the process released energy (exothermic); if the water cools down, the process absorbed energy (endothermic). Dissolve salts in a cup of water, watch the temperature, and practice turning observations into the system-and-surroundings explanation the exam asks for.

2. Key ideas

Draw the boundary

System and surroundings

The system is the part you are studying: the reacting or dissolving particles, or the substance changing phase. The surroundings are everything else, usually the water, the container, and the thermometer. Every energy statement is about one side of this boundary.

The clue

Temperature change signals energy change

The thermometer sits in the surroundings. If it rises, the surroundings gained energy, so the system lost it: exothermic. If it falls, the surroundings lost energy to the system: endothermic. A cold beaker means an endothermic process inside it.

Signs

q and ΔH from the system's view

Energy into the system is positive: endothermic, q > 0, ΔH > 0. Energy out of the system is negative: exothermic, q < 0, ΔH < 0. The surroundings always have the opposite sign: qsurroundings = −qsystem.

First law

Energy is not created or lost

Whatever energy the system loses, the surroundings gain, and the reverse. Energy crosses the boundary as heat or as work. In a coffee-cup experiment almost all of it moves as heat, which is why the water's temperature tracks it.

Dissolving

Can go either way

Separating solute particles and separating solvent molecules both take energy. Forming new solute–solvent attractions releases energy. If the attractions formed are stronger overall than those broken, dissolving is exothermic (CaCl2, NaOH); if weaker, endothermic (NH4NO3, KNO3).

Not only reactions

Physical changes count too

Heating a substance, melting, vaporizing, and sublimation are endothermic for the substance. Cooling, freezing, condensing, and deposition are exothermic. Chemical reactions, phase changes, and heating or cooling are the three main ways a system's energy changes.

4. Endothermic and exothermic on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Cite the evidence first: "The temperature of the solution decreased from 22.0 °C to 15.0 °C."
  • Then name the direction of energy flow across the boundary: "so energy was transferred from the water to the dissolving NH4NO3."
  • Then classify and give the sign: "The dissolution is endothermic; ΔHsoln is positive."
  • For a "why" question about dissolving, compare the attractions: energy required to separate solute particles and water molecules versus energy released when solute–water attractions form.
  • Say which object you mean. "Heat is released" is ambiguous; "the reaction releases energy to the water" is not.
  • For phase changes, connect to particles: melting and boiling absorb energy to overcome intermolecular attractions.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a reaction exothermic because "the beaker lost heat." The beaker is the surroundings; if it got cold, the reaction absorbed energy and is endothermic.
  • Mixing up the sign of q for the water with the sign of q for the reaction. They are always opposite.
  • Saying "cold flows into the hand." Only energy moves, and it moves from the hand into the cold pack.
  • Saying breaking bonds or attractions releases energy. Separating attracted particles always takes energy; forming attractions releases it.
  • Thinking evaporation or melting is exothermic because it happens "on its own." Both absorb energy from the surroundings, which is why sweat cools you.
  • Assuming all dissolving is endothermic, or all is exothermic. The sign depends on the specific solute.
