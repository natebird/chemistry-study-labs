Free Energy of Dissolution Study Lab

Whether a salt dissolves depends on ΔG° for dissolving, which combines three factors: breaking the attractions that hold the solid together, reorganizing the water around the ions, and forming new ion–water attractions. Each factor has its own enthalpy and entropy change, and they largely cancel, so the overall result is hard to predict. Step through the process, then compare real salts.

2. Key ideas

Step 1

Break solute–solute attractions

Pulling the ions out of the lattice takes energy (ΔH > 0, often hundreds of kJ/mol) and frees them from fixed positions (ΔS > 0). Small, highly charged ions are held most strongly.

Step 2

Reorganize the solvent

Some hydrogen bonds between water molecules must break to make room (ΔH > 0). Water molecules then line up around each ion in a hydration shell, which restricts their motion (ΔS < 0). The ordering is strongest around small or highly charged ions such as Li⁺, Ca²⁺, F⁻, and CO₃²⁻.

Step 3

Form solute–solvent attractions

Ion–dipole attractions form: the partially negative oxygen of water points toward cations, and the partially positive hydrogens point toward anions. This releases a large amount of energy (ΔH < 0).

Cancellation

Big numbers, small difference

For NaCl, breaking the lattice costs about 787 kJ/mol and hydrating the ions releases about 783 kJ/mol, so ΔH°soln is only +3.9 kJ/mol. Because large terms nearly cancel, you usually cannot predict the sign of ΔG°soln just by reasoning about the steps.

Two drivers

Enthalpy-driven or entropy-driven

Some salts dissolve because the process is exothermic (CaCl₂ gets hot: ΔH° < 0 outweighs ΔS° < 0). Others dissolve because ΔS° > 0 outweighs an endothermic ΔH° (NH₄NO₃ in a cold pack). Compare the size of ΔH° with TΔS° to tell which.

Temperature

Hotter is not always more soluble

Raising T makes ΔG°soln more negative when ΔS° > 0 and more positive when ΔS° < 0. But whether K for dissolving grows is set by the sign of ΔH°, as Le Châtelier's principle predicts: K increases with T when dissolving is endothermic and decreases when it is exothermic. Li₂CO₃ (ΔH° < 0) is less soluble in hot water.

4. Dissolution on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Name the interactions in each step: ion–ion (solute–solute), hydrogen bonds (solvent–solvent), and ion–dipole (solute–solvent).
  • Give the correct sign for each: breaking attractions is endothermic; forming attractions is exothermic.
  • Use observations: a solution that gets cold means ΔH°soln > 0; one that gets warm means ΔH°soln < 0.
  • If an endothermic dissolution is favored, conclude that ΔS° > 0 and TΔS° > ΔH°.
  • For ΔS° < 0, explain with ordering of water molecules in hydration shells around small or highly charged ions.
  • For a calculation, use ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° with ΔS° in kJ/(mol·K), and connect the sign of ΔG° to whether dissolving is favored.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "bonds break, so energy is released." Separating ions always absorbs energy.
  • Assuming ΔS°soln is always positive. Hydration shells can make it negative.
  • Assuming every salt dissolves better in hot water. Li₂CO₃ dissolves exothermically, and its Ksp decreases as the temperature rises.
  • Claiming that because dissolving is endothermic, it cannot be favored.
  • Pointing the hydrogens of water toward cations in a particle diagram. Oxygen (δ−) faces cations; hydrogen (δ+) faces anions.
  • Predicting ΔG°soln from step-by-step reasoning alone. The terms nearly cancel; use measured values.
