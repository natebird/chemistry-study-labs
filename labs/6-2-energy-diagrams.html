Energy Diagrams Study Lab

An energy diagram is a picture of where a system's energy starts and ends. If the products sit below the reactants, the system lost energy to its surroundings (exothermic); if they sit above, it absorbed energy (endothermic). Build diagrams to scale for real reactions and phase changes, flip them around, and practice reading ΔH off a diagram with no labels.

2. Key ideas

The axes

What the picture shows

The vertical axis is the enthalpy (chemical potential energy) of the system. The horizontal axis is reaction progress: how far the process has gone, not time. Only differences in height have meaning, so the reactant level is often set at zero.

Downhill

Exothermic diagram

Products below reactants. The system ends with less energy than it started with; the difference was released to the surroundings. ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants is negative. Combustion and freezing look like this.

Uphill

Endothermic diagram

Products above reactants. The system had to absorb energy from the surroundings to get there, so ΔH is positive. Melting, vaporizing, decomposing CaCO3, and dissolving NH4NO3 look like this.

Stability

Lower means more stable

Whichever side sits lower on the diagram has less chemical potential energy and is more stable. In an exothermic process the products are more stable; in an endothermic process the reactants are.

Flip it

Reverse the process, flip the sign

Running a process backward follows the same curve in the other direction. The two levels do not move, so ΔH has the same size and the opposite sign: condensation is −ΔHvap, freezing is −ΔHfus.

Don't mix them up

ΔH is not the hump

ΔH depends only on where the curve starts and ends. The height of the hump (the activation energy, topic 5.6) tells you about the path and the rate, not about whether the process is exothermic or endothermic.

Reading a diagram in four moves

  1. Find the reactant level (left plateau) and the product level (right plateau). Ignore the hump for now.
  2. ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants. Read both values off the axis and subtract in that order.
  3. Negative ΔH (downhill): exothermic, energy released, products more stable. Positive ΔH (uphill): endothermic, energy absorbed, reactants more stable.
  4. If asked about the reverse process, keep the size of ΔH and flip the sign.

4. Energy diagrams on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Label the vertical axis with a quantity and units, such as "Enthalpy (kJ/mol)" or "Potential energy," and the horizontal axis "Reaction progress."
  • Draw the product level on the correct side of the reactant level: below for negative ΔH, above for positive ΔH.
  • Draw it to scale when numbers are given. If ΔH = −890 kJ/mol and the hump rises 200 kJ/mol above the reactants, the products must sit about four and a half times farther below the reactant level than the peak sits above it.
  • Mark ΔH with an arrow from the reactant level to the product level, not from the top of the hump.
  • Label each plateau with formulas and states, such as H2O(l), so the grader can see which side is which.
  • When asked to justify "exothermic," point at the diagram: "The products are lower in enthalpy than the reactants, so energy is released to the surroundings."

Common mistakes

  • Calculating ΔH as reactants − products, which gives the wrong sign.
  • Reading the height of the hump as ΔH. The hump is the activation energy and has nothing to do with the sign of ΔH.
  • Thinking a curve that goes down after the peak means the reaction is exothermic. Every curve goes down after its peak; compare the end to the start.
  • Putting time on the horizontal axis. It is reaction progress.
  • Forgetting to flip the sign when the process is reversed or when going from vaporization to condensation.
  • Saying products "have more heat." Heat is energy in transfer; the diagram shows the system's chemical potential energy (enthalpy).
