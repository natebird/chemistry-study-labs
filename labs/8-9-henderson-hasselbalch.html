2. Key ideas

The equation

pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA])

It is the Ka expression rearranged and written in logs. It is given on the equations sheet. You need to use it, not derive it.

Ratio of 1

Equimolar buffer: pH = pKa

When [A−] = [HA], log 1 = 0 and the pH equals the pKa. This is also the half-equivalence point of a weak acid titration, a common way to measure pKa.

Direction

More base, higher pH

[A−] > [HA] gives a positive log and pH > pKa. [HA] > [A−] gives pH < pKa. Each factor of 10 in the ratio moves the pH by 1 unit.

Moles are fine

The volume cancels

Both members are in the same solution, so [A−]/[HA] = nA−/nHA. When a buffer is made by mixing or partial neutralization, work in moles and skip the concentrations.

Choosing a pair

Pick pKa near the target pH

A buffer works best within about one pH unit of its pKa (ratio between 1 : 10 and 10 : 1). For pH 7.4, H2PO4−/HPO42− (pKa 7.21) is a good choice; acetate (4.74) is not.

Weak bases

Use the conjugate acid's pKa

For NH3/NH4+, the acid is NH4+: pH = pKa(NH4+) + log([NH3]/[NH4+]), with pKa = 14.00 − pKb = 9.26.

Buffer made by partial neutralization

  1. Write the reaction: HA + OH− → A− + H2O. It goes to completion.
  2. Moles of A− formed = moles of OH− added. Moles of HA left = initial moles of HA − moles of OH−.
  3. If HA is left over and some A− formed, it is a buffer: pH = pKa + log(nA−/nHA).
  4. If exactly half the HA was neutralized, nA− = nHA and pH = pKa.

4. Henderson–Hasselbalch on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the equation with the numbers substituted: "pH = 4.74 + log(0.10/0.20) = 4.44."
  • Put the conjugate base on top. For an ammonia buffer, NH3 is the base and goes in the numerator.
  • Show the pKa calculation when given Ka or Kb: pKa = −log(1.8 × 10−5) = 4.74.
  • Check the direction: if base exceeds acid, your pH must be above the pKa.
  • For a half-equivalence point, say [HA] = [A−] so pH = pKa, then Ka = 10−pH.
  • Report pH to two decimal places when the data have two significant figures (the digits after the decimal point in pH are the significant ones).

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the ratio: log([HA]/[A−]) gives a pH on the wrong side of the pKa.
  • Using pKb in place of pKa for an ammonia buffer, which gives a pH near 5 instead of 9.
  • Using the initial moles of acid instead of the moles left after NaOH reacts.
  • Applying the equation when only one member is present (a weak acid alone, or the equivalence point). It is a buffer equation.
  • Thinking that diluting a buffer changes its pH. The ratio, and therefore the pH, stays the same.
  • Using ln instead of log.
