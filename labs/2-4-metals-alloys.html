2. Key ideas

Model

Electron-sea model

Metal atoms have low ionization energies and give up their valence electrons to the whole sample. The positive metal cores sit in a lattice surrounded by a sea of delocalized electrons. The bonding is the attraction between the cores and that sea.

Conductivity

Why metals conduct

The delocalized electrons are free to move through the lattice. Apply a voltage and they drift toward the positive terminal, carrying current in the solid. Mobile electrons also make metals good conductors of heat.

Malleable, ductile

Why metals bend

When layers of metal cores slide past one another, the electron sea moves with them and keeps holding the cores together. No like charges are forced together, so the metal deforms instead of breaking (compare ionic solids in 2.3).

Similar sizes

Substitutional alloys

When the added atoms are about the same size as the host atoms, they take the place of host atoms in the lattice. Brass is zinc substituted into copper. Density and other properties are typically between those of the component metals, and the alloy stays malleable and ductile.

Much smaller atoms

Interstitial alloys

When the added atoms are much smaller, they fit into the holes (interstices) between host atoms. Steel is carbon in the spaces of an iron lattice. The small atoms pin the layers in place, making the lattice more rigid: stronger and harder, less malleable and ductile.

Still metallic

Alloys keep the electron sea

Alloys still have mobile valence electrons, so they still conduct electricity and heat and still look metallic. Atoms of a second element disrupt the regular lattice, so conductivity is usually somewhat lower than the pure metal's.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Describe metallic bonding as positive metal cores (cations) in a sea of delocalized valence electrons.
  • Explain conductivity with mobile electrons: "the delocalized electrons can move through the solid."
  • Explain malleability: layers of cations can slide past each other while the electron sea keeps holding them together.
  • Classify an alloy by comparing atomic radii: similar radii → substitutional; much smaller added atom → interstitial.
  • For an interstitial alloy, say that the small atoms in the holes interfere with layers sliding, making the alloy less malleable and ductile.
  • In particle diagrams, keep the host lattice regular: replace host atoms (substitutional) or put smaller atoms in the gaps (interstitial).

Common mistakes

  • Saying metals conduct because of mobile ions. In a solid metal the cores stay in place; the electrons move.
  • Calling steel substitutional or brass interstitial. Check the sizes: Zn is about the size of Cu; C is much smaller than Fe.
  • Saying an alloy is a compound with a fixed formula. Alloys are mixtures with variable composition.
  • Saying alloys stop conducting. They keep the electron sea; conductivity is just usually lower.
  • Predicting a substitutional alloy's density outside the range of its components. It typically lies between them.
  • Drawing interstitial atoms larger than the host atoms, or drawing them replacing host atoms.
