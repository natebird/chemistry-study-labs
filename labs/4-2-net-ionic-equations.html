Net Ionic Equations Study Lab

When two ionic solutions are mixed, most of the ions just float around: only the ions that form a solid, water, or a gas actually react. A net ionic equation keeps those ions and drops the spectators. Mix pairs of solutions, watch the particles, and build the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations side by side.

2. Key ideas

Three forms

Molecular, complete ionic, net ionic

The molecular equation writes every compound as a whole formula. The complete ionic equation splits every strong electrolyte in solution into its ions. The net ionic equation removes ions that appear unchanged on both sides. All three describe the same reaction; which one you use depends on what the question asks.

Must know

The one solubility rule

All compounds containing sodium, potassium, ammonium, or nitrate ions are soluble in water. If a product contains one of these ions, it stays dissolved. For any other compound, the exam tells you whether it is soluble.

Split into ions

What gets written as ions

Soluble ionic compounds (aq), strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄), and strong bases (Group 1 hydroxides and Ca, Sr, Ba hydroxides) are written as separated ions. Everything else stays together: solids, liquids, gases, water, weak acids, weak bases, and molecular substances.

Spectators

Spectator ions

A spectator ion is in solution before and after the reaction in the same form. It is still physically present in the beaker; it is just not part of the change. Cancel only ions that are identical on both sides: same formula, same charge, same state.

Driving forces

When ions actually react

Ions react when they can form something that leaves solution: an insoluble solid (precipitate), a molecular substance such as water, or a gas such as CO₂. If every possible product is soluble, all ions are spectators and there is no net ionic equation.

Check twice

Balance atoms and charge

A correct net ionic equation is balanced for atoms of each element and for total charge. For Pb²⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂(s), the left side has a total charge of (+2) + 2(−1) = 0, matching the neutral solid. Use the smallest whole-number coefficients.

4. Answering the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the formula of each product correctly first: swap partners, then use charges to set subscripts (Pb²⁺ and I⁻ make PbI₂, not PbI).
  • Show states. A net ionic equation with (aq) on the reactant ions and (s), (l), or (g) on the product shows why the reaction happens.
  • Keep polyatomic ions together: SO₄²⁻ stays SO₄²⁻, never S + 4O.
  • Check both atom balance and charge balance, and reduce to the smallest whole-number coefficients (2Ag⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → 2AgCl reduces to Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl).
  • When asked to identify spectator ions, name them with charges and explain that they are present in the same form before and after the reaction.
  • For a particle diagram after precipitation, draw the solid as a cluster of ions at the bottom and the spectator ions still dispersed in solution, in the correct numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Splitting a precipitate, water, or a weak acid into ions. Only strong electrolytes in solution are split.
  • Leaving a strong acid as a molecule: HNO₃(aq) must be written as H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq).
  • Carrying the subscript of a compound into the ion's coefficient incorrectly: Ca(NO₃)₂ gives Ca²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻, not NO₃²⁻ or N₂O₆.
  • Writing a net ionic equation when every product is soluble. The answer is "no reaction"; all ions are spectators.
  • Saying spectator ions leave the solution. They stay in the beaker; they just do not change.
  • Forgetting that a solid reactant (like a metal or CaCO₃(s)) stays as a whole formula even if it is ionic.
