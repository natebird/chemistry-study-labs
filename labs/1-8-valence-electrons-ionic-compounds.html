Valence Electrons and Ionic Compounds Study Lab

An element's column on the periodic table tells you how many valence electrons it has, and that predicts the ion it usually forms: metals lose their valence electrons, nonmetals gain enough to fill their p subshell. Because an ionic compound has no net charge, those ion charges fix its formula. Pick a metal and a nonmetal, watch each become an ion, and add ions until the charges balance.

2. Key ideas

Group number

Counting valence electrons

For main-group elements, valence electrons are the s and p electrons in the outermost shell. Group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, and groups 13–18 have the group number minus 10 (3 through 8). Helium is the exception: 2. Every element in a column has the same valence configuration, such as ns² np⁴ for group 16.

Metals → cations

Lose the valence electrons

Metals on the left have few valence electrons and low ionization energies, so they lose them: group 1 forms 1+, group 2 forms 2+, aluminum forms 3+. The charge stops there because the next electron would come from a core shell, much closer to the nucleus and far harder to remove (the big jump in successive ionization energies).

Nonmetals → anions

Gain electrons to fill the p subshell

Nonmetals on the right gain electrons until the valence s and p subshells are full: group 17 forms 1−, group 16 forms 2−, and N and P form 3−. One more electron would have to go into the next, farther shell, so the charge is 8 minus the number of valence electrons.

Isoelectronic

Same number of electrons

Most main-group ions end up with the same electron configuration as a noble gas. N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Al³⁺ all have 10 electrons, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, like neon: they are isoelectronic. Among them, more protons means a smaller ion (topic 1.7).

Same column

Analogous compounds

Elements in a column have the same number of valence electrons, so they form ions of the same charge and compounds with the same ratio. NaCl and KBr are both 1:1; MgO and CaS are both 1:1; Al₂O₃ and Ga₂S₃ are both 2:3. If you know one formula, you can predict the formula for its column-mates, such as RbF or BaSe.

Charge balance

Writing the formula

An ionic compound has no net charge, so total positive charge equals total negative charge. The formula unit is the lowest whole-number ratio of ions: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0, so Al₂O₃. Always reduce: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ give MgO, not Mg₂O₂. Transition-metal charges are not predicted from position in this topic.

4. Ions and formulas on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the number of valence electrons and tie it to position: "Ca is in group 2, so it has two valence electrons (4s²)."
  • Say what happens to them: a metal loses its valence electrons to form a cation; a nonmetal gains electrons to fill its valence p subshell.
  • Show the charge balance explicitly: 3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0 for Mg₃N₂.
  • To justify an analogous formula, cite the shared column: "S is in the same group as O, so it also forms a 2− ion; CaS is analogous to CaO."
  • For the ion's configuration, remove (or add) electrons from the valence shell and name the noble gas it matches when it does.
  • If asked why the charge stops (why not Na²⁺), point to the next electron coming from a core shell, which is much closer to the nucleus and needs far more energy.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the subscripts: Mg₂N₃ instead of Mg₃N₂. Check by multiplying each charge by its subscript.
  • Not reducing the ratio: Ca₂O₂ instead of CaO. A formula unit is the lowest whole-number ratio.
  • Writing charges in the formula (Al₂³⁺O₃²⁻) or listing the anion first.
  • Saying an atom "wants" a full octet. Explain with electrons lost or gained and the energy cost of the next one.
  • Giving group 15 a 5+ charge or group 13 a 3− charge. Metals form cations, nonmetals form anions.
  • Calling two species isoelectronic because they are in the same group. Isoelectronic means the same number of electrons (Na⁺ and F⁻), not the same column (Na and K).
