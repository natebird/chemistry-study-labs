Properties of Photons Study Lab

Light comes in packets called photons. When an atom or molecule absorbs a photon, its energy rises by exactly the photon's energy; when it emits one, its energy falls by the same amount. Two equations connect a photon's energy, frequency and wavelength: E = hν and c = λν. Move electrons between energy levels, then see why only photons with enough energy can knock electrons out of a metal.

B. Light on a metal: one photon, one electron

Each electron in a metal is held with a minimum energy, the threshold energy. A single photon either has at least that much energy or it does not. Change the color and the brightness of the light and watch what comes off. Threshold energies are approximate literature values. 2. Key ideas

c = λν

Wavelength and frequency

All light travels at c = 2.998 × 108 m/s in a vacuum. Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: halve the wavelength and the frequency doubles.

E = hν

Planck's equation

The energy of one photon is proportional to its frequency: E = hν, with h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s. Combine the two equations to get E = hc/λ: shorter wavelength, more energy.

Conservation

Photon energy = level gap

When an atom absorbs a photon, its energy rises by exactly the photon's energy. When it drops to a lower level, it emits a photon whose energy equals the drop. A photon whose energy matches no gap is not absorbed.

Line spectra

Why atoms give lines

Because the energy levels are quantized, only certain energy differences, and therefore certain wavelengths, can be emitted or absorbed. Each element has its own set of lines.

Per photon vs. per mole

Scaling up

E = hν gives joules for one photon. Multiply by Avogadro's number for a mole of photons, then divide by 1000 for kJ/mol, which you can compare with bond energies.

Intensity

Brighter is not stronger

Brightness is the number of photons, not the energy of each one. If a single photon cannot eject an electron from a metal, a billion of them cannot either. Above the threshold, brighter light ejects more electrons, not faster ones.

4. Answering it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the equation, substitute with units, and give the answer with units and three significant figures.
  • For a transition, state that the photon's energy equals the difference between the two levels.
  • Say which direction: a drop to a lower level emits a photon; a jump to a higher level requires absorbing one.
  • Compare photons with the relationships, not just numbers: "shorter wavelength, so higher frequency and higher energy per photon."
  • Connect the photon's energy to the kind of transition (topic 3.11): UV-visible for electronic, infrared for vibrational, microwave for rotational.
  • When asked for energy per mole, multiply by Avogadro's number and convert J to kJ.

Common mistakes

  • Plugging nanometers into c = λν. 650 nm is 6.50 × 10−7 m.
  • Inverting the relationship: longer wavelength means lower energy.
  • Thinking higher-energy light travels faster. Every photon moves at c.
  • Reporting a negative photon energy. The level difference can be negative; the photon's energy is its size.
  • Claiming brighter light ejects faster electrons. Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons, with the same maximum kinetic energy.
  • Mixing up per photon and per mole when comparing with a bond energy in kJ/mol.
