Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy Study Lab

Two atoms form a bond because their potential energy is lower together than apart. The potential energy curve shows that balance between attraction and repulsion: its lowest point gives the bond length and the bond energy. Move two atoms together, compare curves for different bonds, then use Coulomb's law to compare ionic attractions.

Part B: Coulomb's law for ions

In an ionic bond the attraction is between whole charges. Coulomb's law says the potential energy of two charges is proportional to q₁q₂ / r, where r is the distance between the ion centers (here, the sum of the two ionic radii). Larger charges and smaller ions mean a stronger attraction. Values are relative to Na⁺ with Cl⁻; ionic radii are standard tabulated values for six-coordinate ions.

2. Key ideas

Zero point

Separated atoms have zero potential energy

When the atoms are far apart they do not interact, so the curve is flat at zero on the right side. Every point below zero means the pair is more stable than separate atoms.

Downhill

Attraction lowers the energy

As the atoms approach, each nucleus attracts the other atom's electrons. The energy drops. A bond forms because the bonded pair has lower potential energy than the separate atoms, so forming a bond releases energy.

Steep wall

Repulsion takes over up close

Pushed too close, the two positive nuclei (and the two electron clouds) repel strongly. The energy shoots up on the left side of the curve. The atoms cannot get closer than the bond length without a large energy cost.

The minimum

Bond length and bond energy

The bottom of the well is where attraction and repulsion balance. Its distance is the bond length. Its depth below zero is the bond energy, the energy needed to break the bond and separate the atoms.

Size and bond order

What moves the minimum

Larger atoms make longer, usually weaker bonds (H–F to H–I). A higher bond order between the same two atoms (single, double, triple) puts more shared electrons between the nuclei, so the bond is shorter and stronger.

Coulomb's law

Ionic attraction

For ions, potential energy is proportional to q₁q₂/r. Doubling both charges multiplies the attraction by four; smaller ions sit closer together. Charge usually matters more than size: MgO is far stronger than NaF.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Read the bond length from the x-coordinate of the minimum and the bond energy from how far the minimum is below zero.
  • Explain the left side of the curve with repulsion between the two nuclei (and between the electron clouds) when the atoms are too close.
  • Explain the well with attraction between each nucleus and the shared electrons, which lowers the potential energy.
  • When sketching a curve for a larger atom, move the minimum to the right and make it shallower; for a higher bond order, move it left and make it deeper.
  • For ions, cite Coulomb's law: attraction increases with the magnitude of the charges and decreases with the distance between ion centers.
  • When comparing ionic compounds, compare charges first, then ionic radii, and name the specific ions.

Common mistakes

  • Saying energy is needed to form a bond. Forming a bond releases energy; breaking a bond requires energy.
  • Reading the bond length where the curve crosses zero on the left. That crossing is closer than the bond length; the bond length is at the minimum.
  • Saying the atoms stop at the bond length because there is no force. Both attraction and repulsion are present; they balance there.
  • Assuming each extra bond adds the same energy, so a triple bond is exactly three times a single bond. Bond energy rises with bond order, but not in equal steps (C≡C 839 is less than 3 × 348; N≡N 941 is more than 3 × 163).
  • Using only size to compare ionic attractions when the charges differ. A 2+/2− pair beats a 1+/1− pair even with larger ions.
  • Writing Coulomb's law with r² for potential energy. Force goes as 1/r²; potential energy goes as 1/r. AP questions only need the direction of the trend.
