Representations of Solutions Study Lab

The AP exam asks you to draw and judge particle diagrams of solutions. A correct diagram keeps the formula's ion ratio, keeps polyatomic ions and molecules in one piece, and turns each water molecule the right way: the partially negative oxygen toward a cation, a partially positive hydrogen toward an anion. Build diagrams, then hunt for the mistakes in flawed ones.

2. Key ideas

Ion–dipole

Water turns to face each ion

Water is polar: the oxygen end carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and the hydrogen ends carry partial positive charges (δ+). Opposite charges attract, so oxygen points toward cations and hydrogen points toward anions.

Ratio

Keep the formula's ratio

CaCl2 must show two Cl− for every Ca2+; Na2SO4 must show two Na+ for every SO42−. Count the ions in your drawing: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

Separated

Dissolved ions are apart

A soluble ionic compound dissociates. In the diagram, cations and anions should be separate, each surrounded by water, not drawn touching as in the solid.

Intact

Polyatomic ions stay whole

SO42−, NO3−, NH4+ and other polyatomic ions are held together by covalent bonds. Dissolving breaks the ionic attractions between ions, not the covalent bonds inside an ion.

Molecular

Molecules stay molecules

Glucose and methanol dissolve as whole molecules. Water forms hydrogen bonds to their −OH groups, but no ions form, which is why these solutions do not conduct electricity.

Charge size

Bigger charge, tighter shell

By Coulomb's law, a small ion with a large charge (Al3+, Mg2+) attracts water more strongly than a large ion with a small charge (K+). Use that when an FRQ asks which ion has the stronger ion–dipole attraction.

4. Drawing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Draw at least one water molecule next to each kind of ion, with the correct end pointing at it. Graders look at orientation first.
  • Make the ion counts match the formula and any stated amounts (for example, 2 Na+ and 1 SO42− per formula unit).
  • Label each particle with its formula and charge, and include a key if you use symbols.
  • If two solutions are compared, scale the number of solute particles to the concentration (twice the molarity, twice the particles in the same volume).
  • Explain orientation with charges: "the partially negative O end of water is attracted to the positive Na+ ion."
  • Draw cations smaller than anions of the same period when sizes matter (Na+ smaller than Cl−).

Common mistakes

  • Hydrogens pointing at a cation, or oxygen pointing at an anion.
  • Drawing ions paired up or touching, as they are in the solid.
  • Splitting SO42− into S and O atoms, or NO3− into N and O.
  • Drawing a molecular solute (sugar, alcohol) as ions.
  • Using a 1 : 1 ratio for every compound; CaCl2 and Na2SO4 are 1 : 2 and 2 : 1.
  • Saying water's O is attracted to an anion "because O is electronegative". The O end is partially negative, so it is repelled by an anion.
