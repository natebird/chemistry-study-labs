Mass Spectra of Elements Study Lab

Most elements are a mix of isotopes: atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. A mass spectrometer sorts the atoms by mass and shows how common each isotope is. The atomic mass on the periodic table is the weighted average of those isotopes. Pick an element, change the abundances, and see how the average moves.

2. Key ideas

Same Z, different A

Isotopes

Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons (so they are the same element) but different numbers of neutrons, so their masses differ. Chlorine-35 has 17 protons and 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 has 17 protons and 20 neutrons.

Reading the graph

What a mass spectrum shows

The horizontal axis is the mass of each isotope (in amu, or mass-to-charge ratio). The height of each peak is that isotope's relative abundance. The number of peaks is the number of naturally occurring isotopes.

Σ mass × fraction

Weighted average

Average atomic mass = Σ (mass of isotope × fractional abundance). Convert percents to decimals first (75.76% → 0.7576). The average always lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope.

Closer to the tallest peak

Estimate before you calculate

The average is pulled toward the most abundant isotope; with two isotopes it always sits closer to the more abundant one. Chlorine is mostly Cl-35, so its average (35.45) is much nearer 35 than 37. Bromine is about half Br-79 and half Br-81, so its average (79.90) sits near the middle.

No atom weighs 35.45 amu

The average is not a real atom

Every chlorine atom has a mass near 35 or near 37 amu. The 35.45 on the periodic table describes a large natural sample, which is why it is the number used for molar mass.

x and 1 − x

Solving for an abundance

For two isotopes, let x be the fraction of one: average = m₁x + m₂(1 − x). Solve for x. The two fractions must add to 1, so the other isotope is 1 − x.

4. Mass spectra on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the setup: (34.97)(0.7576) + (36.97)(0.2424) = 35.45 amu. Show each product, then the sum.
  • Convert percents to decimals, and check that the fractions add to 1 (or the percents to 100).
  • When asked to explain why the average is closer to one mass, say that isotope is more abundant, so it contributes more to the weighted average.
  • To identify an element, compute the weighted average and match it to the periodic table. Identity comes from the average mass here, because the spectrum does not show the number of protons directly.
  • For an unknown abundance, set up average = m₁x + m₂(1 − x), solve for x, and report both percents.
  • Explain different peaks with "different numbers of neutrons." Same element means same number of protons.

Common mistakes

  • Taking a simple average of the isotope masses. (35 + 37) ÷ 2 = 36, not 35.45: the abundances matter.
  • Multiplying by the percent instead of the fraction, which gives an answer 100 times too large.
  • Saying isotopes differ in protons or electrons. A different number of protons is a different element.
  • Reading the tallest peak as the average atomic mass. The tallest peak is the most common isotope.
  • Assuming an element has one isotope because its atomic mass is close to a whole number. Check the spectrum.
  • Swapping x and 1 − x when solving for abundance. Check: the more abundant isotope must be the one closer to the average.
