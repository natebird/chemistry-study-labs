2. Key ideas

Same event, two forms

Symbols and particles

A balanced equation can be translated into a particle diagram and back. Coefficients become numbers of molecules (in the same ratio), formulas become how atoms are bonded together, and states become how the particles are arranged.

Count atoms

Conservation in a diagram

Every atom in the before box must appear in the after box, bonded in a new way or unchanged. Count each element before and after. If the counts differ, the diagram is wrong no matter how good the products look.

Molecules are not conserved

The number of particles can change

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, three molecules become two. A diagram can have fewer (or more) particles after the reaction as long as every atom is accounted for.

Excess stays

Limiting and excess reactants

Divide each reactant's count by its coefficient; the smallest result is the limiting reactant and the number of times the reaction can happen. The other reactant's leftovers must still be drawn in the after box, unchanged.

Diagram to equation

Reading an equation off a diagram

Count what was used and what was made, ignore leftover reactant (it appears on both sides), and reduce to the smallest whole numbers. Four H₂ and two O₂ used to make four H₂O reduces to 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

States matter

Arrangement shows phase

Gas particles are far apart, liquid particles are close but disordered, solid particles are packed in a regular pattern. Ions in solution are drawn separated; a precipitate is drawn as a cluster of ions. Molecules in a phase change stay intact.

3. Checklist for a particle diagram

Run every diagram you draw or judge through these checks. Example column uses 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 5 H₂ and 2 O₂ before.

CheckWhat to look forExample
Atoms of each elementSame count before and afterH: 10 before, 10 after. O: 4 before, 4 after.
Limiting reactantSmallest (molecules ÷ coefficient)H₂: 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5. O₂: 2 ÷ 1 = 2. O₂ is limiting, so the reaction happens 2 times.
Product amountEvents × product coefficient2 × 2 = 4 H₂O molecules
Leftover reactantStart − events × coefficientH₂: 5 − 2 × 2 = 1 H₂ left. O₂: 2 − 2 × 1 = 0.
Correct formulasAtoms bonded as in the formula, not floating separatelyEach H₂O is one O bonded to two H, not an H₂ next to an O
Phase and spacingGas spread out; liquid close; solid ordered; aqueous ions separatedH₂O(g) molecules spread out; H₂O(l) drawn close together

4. Answering the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Draw the correct number of each product particle and the correct number of leftover reactant particles. Graders count them.
  • Draw molecules as bonded atoms in the right ratio (an NH₃ is one N touching three H), using the key the question gives.
  • Keep the same representation for the same atom in both boxes (same size, same shading).
  • For ionic solutions, show separated ions with the right charges and ratio (two Cl⁻ for every Ca²⁺ from CaCl₂); show a precipitate as a cluster of ions.
  • When writing an equation from a diagram, leave out the unreacted particles and reduce the coefficients.
  • If asked to justify, cite atom counts: "There are 10 H atoms and 4 O atoms in both boxes, so atoms are conserved."

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out the excess reactant from the after box.
  • Drawing the same number of molecules after as before. Atoms, not molecules, are conserved.
  • Using the coefficients themselves as the number of particles to draw, regardless of how many were in the before box.
  • Drawing a partial reaction, with both reactants left over, for a reaction that goes to completion.
  • Drawing a diatomic element as single atoms (O instead of O₂) or splitting a polyatomic ion into atoms.
  • Writing an equation from the diagram that includes the leftover reactant on both sides.
