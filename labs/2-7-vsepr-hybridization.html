2. Key ideas

Count domains

Electron domains

An electron domain is any region of electrons around the central atom: a single bond, a double bond, a triple bond, or a lone pair. Each counts as one domain. Get the count from the Lewis diagram, never from the formula alone.

Two geometries

Electron vs. molecular geometry

The electron-domain geometry describes where all domains point. The molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are. NH3 has a tetrahedral electron geometry but a trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry, because one domain is a lone pair.

Squeezed angles

Lone pairs repel more

A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out and repels more strongly than a bonding pair. Bond angles shrink: 109.5° in CH4, about 107° in NH3, about 104.5° in H2O.

Hybridization

sp, sp², sp³

2 domains: sp hybridized, 180°. 3 domains: sp², 120°. 4 domains: sp³, 109.5°. Hybridization involving d orbitals (5 and 6 domains) is not assessed on the AP exam.

σ and π

Sigma and pi bonds

Every single bond is a σ bond (end-to-end overlap). A double bond is one σ + one π; a triple bond is one σ + two π. π bonds come from side-by-side overlap, are weaker than σ bonds, and prevent rotation around a double bond.

Dipoles

Polar molecules

A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel. Symmetric shapes with identical outer atoms (linear AX2, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, square planar) are nonpolar. Lone pairs on the central atom usually make the shape asymmetric and the molecule polar.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Start from the Lewis diagram and state the count: "the central N has four electron domains: three bonding pairs and one lone pair."
  • Name the molecular geometry, not just the electron geometry, when the question asks for the shape of the molecule.
  • Justify a bond angle with repulsion: "the lone pair repels the bonding pairs more strongly than they repel each other, so the angle is less than 109.5°."
  • Justify polarity with both bond polarity and shape: "the C–F bonds are polar, but the tetrahedral shape makes the dipoles cancel, so CF4 is nonpolar."
  • Connect hybridization to domains: 4 domains → sp³, 3 → sp², 2 → sp.
  • Count σ and π bonds from the Lewis diagram: every bond has exactly one σ; extra bonds in a double or triple bond are π.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a double or triple bond as two or three domains. It is one domain.
  • Calling NH3 "tetrahedral" or H2O "linear." Lone pairs set the electron geometry but are not part of the molecular shape.
  • Saying a molecule with polar bonds must be polar. Symmetry can cancel the dipoles (CO2, BF3, CCl4, XeF4).
  • Forgetting the lone pairs on the central atom when they come from an expanded octet (SF4, XeF2, XeF4).
  • Putting lone pairs in axial positions of a trigonal bipyramid. They go equatorial, where they have more room.
  • Writing a bond angle of exactly 109.5° for NH3 or H2O. Write "less than 109.5°."
