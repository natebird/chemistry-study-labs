2. Key ideas

Water reacts with itself

Autoionization and Kw

One water molecule can give a proton to another: 2H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). The equilibrium constant is Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C. Water is a pure liquid, so it does not appear in the expression. This equilibrium is present in every aqueous solution, not only in pure water.

Logarithms

pH and pOH

pH = −log[H₃O⁺] and pOH = −log[OH⁻]. The minus sign means a higher concentration gives a lower number. Because the scale is logarithmic, each change of 1 pH unit is a tenfold change in [H₃O⁺]; a change of 3 units is a factor of 1000.

The seesaw

pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C

Taking −log of both sides of Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] gives pKw = pH + pOH. At 25 °C, pKw = 14.00. Raising [H₃O⁺] tenfold must lower [OH⁻] tenfold, so the product stays the same. Neither ion ever falls to zero.

Definition

Acidic, neutral, basic

The definitions compare the two ions: acidic means [H₃O⁺] > [OH⁻], neutral means [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻], basic means [H₃O⁺] < [OH⁻]. At 25 °C those match pH < 7, pH = 7, and pH > 7, but the pH cutoffs are a consequence, not the definition.

Temperature

Kw changes with temperature

Autoionization is endothermic, so Kw grows as water warms. At 50 °C, Kw ≈ 5.5 × 10−14, and pure water has pH ≈ 6.63. It is still neutral, because both ions increased by the same amount. Only at 25 °C is neutral exactly pH 7.00.

Notation

H⁺ or H₃O⁺, and decimal places

H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) mean the same ion; hydronium is preferred, and both are accepted on the AP Exam. For logs, the number of decimal places in pH equals the number of significant figures in [H₃O⁺]: 2.5 × 10−4 M gives pH 3.60, not 3.6 or 3.602.

4. pH and Kw on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the relationship before plugging in: "pH = −log[H₃O⁺] = −log(2.5 × 10−4) = 3.60."
  • To go from pH to concentration, undo the log: [H₃O⁺] = 10−pH. Show it as a separate step.
  • Use Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] or pH + pOH = 14.00 to get the other ion; say "at 25 °C" when you use 14.00.
  • Justify "neutral" with [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻], not with "pH = 7," especially when the temperature is not 25 °C.
  • For a temperature question, connect the trend to the equilibrium: Kw increases with temperature, so autoionization is endothermic, and both [H₃O⁺] and [OH⁻] increase in pure water.
  • Compare two solutions by their pH difference: a difference of n units means a factor of 10n in [H₃O⁺].

Common mistakes

  • Reporting pOH when the question asks for pH (or the reverse). Check: a solution with excess OH⁻ must have pH above 7 at 25 °C.
  • Saying a basic solution "has no H₃O⁺." Both ions are always present; their product is Kw.
  • Thinking pH 2 is "twice as acidic" as pH 4. It has 100 times the [H₃O⁺].
  • Writing [H₃O⁺] + [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10−14. It is the product, not the sum.
  • Calling warm pure water acidic because its pH is below 7. It is neutral at any temperature.
  • Keeping too many digits: 4.0 × 10−8 M has two significant figures, so pH = 7.40.
