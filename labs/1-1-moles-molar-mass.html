    Building the molar mass

    2. Key ideas

    NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

    The mole is a counting unit

    A mole is 6.022 × 1023 particles, the same way a dozen is 12. The particles can be atoms, molecules, formula units, ions, or electrons, so always say which one you are counting.

    amu ↔ g/mol

    Why molar mass works

    The mole is defined so that the average mass of one particle in atomic mass units (amu) equals the mass of one mole of those particles in grams. One H₂O molecule averages 18.02 amu, so one mole of water is 18.02 g.

    n = m / M

    Grams to moles

    Divide mass by molar mass to get moles. Multiply moles by molar mass to get grams. Molar mass is the sum of the atomic masses of every atom in the formula, counting subscripts and anything in parentheses.

    N = n × NA

    Moles to particles

    Multiply moles by Avogadro's number to get the number of particles. Grams never convert straight to particles: you always pass through moles first.

    Subscripts

    Atoms inside particles

    One molecule of C₆H₁₂O₆ holds 6 C, 12 H, and 6 O atoms. So the moles (or number) of H atoms is 12 times the moles (or number) of glucose molecules. Subscripts are mole ratios inside a formula.

    Equal masses ≠ equal counts

    Same mass, different moles

    Ten grams of H₂ and ten grams of O₂ weigh the same but hold very different numbers of molecules. The substance with the smaller molar mass has more moles, and so more particles, in the same mass.

    4. Mole calculations on the FRQ

    What earns points on the FRQ

    • Show the setup with units, such as 36.0 g × (1 mol / 18.02 g). A correct number with no work often earns no credit.
    • Write the molar mass you used. If you add it up yourself, use the masses from the periodic table they give you.
    • Name the particle: "molecules of H₂O" or "atoms of H," not just "particles."
    • Round only at the end, to the number of significant figures in the measured data (usually three).
    • For "identify the element," compute molar mass as grams ÷ moles and name the element whose mass is closest.
    • For mole comparisons ("which sample has more atoms?"), convert each sample to moles of the thing being counted before comparing.

    Common mistakes

    • Multiplying grams by molar mass when you should divide. Check the units: g × g/mol gives g²/mol, which is meaningless.
    • Forgetting the subscript outside parentheses: Ca(OH)₂ has 2 O and 2 H, so its molar mass is 74.10 g/mol, not 58.10.
    • Counting molecules when the question asks for atoms. One mole of CO₂ is 6.022 × 1023 molecules but 1.807 × 1024 atoms.
    • Using the mass number (12, 16) instead of the average atomic mass (12.01, 16.00) from the periodic table.
    • Thinking equal masses mean equal numbers of particles. Equal moles mean equal numbers of particles.
    • Calling 6.022 × 1023 a mass. It is a count; it has units of mol⁻¹.
