2. Key ideas

Resonance

One particle, several diagrams

When two or more Lewis diagrams differ only in where the electrons (multiple bonds and lone pairs) are placed, and the atoms stay in the same positions, they are resonance structures. Draw them all with double-headed arrows (↔) between them.

Hybrid

The real structure is an average

The molecule does not flip between the structures. Its electrons are delocalized, so the actual structure is a single resonance hybrid. In NO3− all three N–O bonds are identical, with a bond order of 1⅓ and a length between a single and a double bond.

Bookkeeping

Formal charge

Formal charge = (valence electrons) − (nonbonding electrons) − ½(bonding electrons). It assigns each atom all of its lone-pair electrons and half of its shared ones. The formal charges always add up to the charge of the species.

Choosing

Rules for the best diagram

Prefer the diagram whose formal charges are closest to zero (smallest total |FC|). If some negative formal charge is needed, it should be on the more electronegative atom. Avoid like charges on neighboring atoms.

Skeletons

Formal charge picks atom arrangements too

N2O is N–N–O, not N–O–N. The N–O–N arrangement forces a +2 formal charge onto oxygen, the most electronegative atom, so it is a poor model. Formal charge helps decide both bonding and connectivity.

Limits

Where Lewis diagrams fail

Lewis diagrams are a model. They cannot give every atom an octet when the total number of valence electrons is odd (NO, NO2), and a single diagram cannot show delocalized electrons; that takes resonance.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • When asked for "all resonance structures," draw each one completely, with every lone pair, connected by double-headed arrows.
  • Show the formal charge calculation for the atom in question: valence − nonbonding − ½ bonding, with numbers.
  • Justify the best structure with formal charge: "all formal charges are zero" or "the −1 formal charge is on O, the most electronegative atom."
  • Use resonance to explain equal bond lengths: "the electrons are delocalized, so all three bonds are identical and intermediate between single and double."
  • State the average bond order as a fraction (1⅓ for NO3−, 1½ for O3).
  • Check that the formal charges add up to the overall charge.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the molecule switches back and forth between resonance structures. It is always the hybrid.
  • Moving atoms between "resonance structures." If the atoms move, the diagrams are different molecules (isomers), not resonance structures.
  • Predicting one short double bond and two long single bonds in NO3−. All three bonds have the same length.
  • Counting all the bonding electrons instead of half when calculating formal charge.
  • Confusing formal charge with oxidation number. Formal charge splits bonding electrons evenly; oxidation number gives them all to the more electronegative atom.
  • Choosing a structure with a negative formal charge on the less electronegative atom when a structure with it on the more electronegative atom exists.
