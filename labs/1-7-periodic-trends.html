2. Key ideas

Across a period →

More protons, same shell

Moving right, each element adds a proton and an electron to the same valence shell. Core shielding stays about the same, so the valence electrons feel a stronger pull: radius shrinks, ionization energy and electronegativity rise.

Down a group ↓

A new shell each period

Moving down, valence electrons occupy a higher shell, farther from the nucleus and shielded by more core electrons. The attraction is weaker even though there are more protons: radius grows, ionization energy and electronegativity fall.

Ionization energy

Energy to remove an electron

First ionization energy is the energy to remove the most loosely held electron from a gaseous atom. Exceptions: B < Be and Al < Mg (the p electron is higher in energy and shielded by the s electrons), and O < N and S < P (a paired p electron is repelled by its partner).

Electronegativity

Pull on shared electrons

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts electrons in a bond. It follows ionization energy: highest at fluorine (3.98), lowest at the bottom of group 1. Noble gases He, Ne, and Ar have no listed value because they form no common compounds.

Ions

Ionic radius

A cation is smaller than its atom (it often loses its whole valence shell, and fewer electrons share the same nuclear charge). An anion is larger than its atom (added electrons repel each other). In an isoelectronic series, more protons means a smaller ion: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺.

Electron affinity

Energy change on gaining an electron

Electron affinity generally becomes more favorable (more energy released) toward the upper right, for the same Coulomb's law reasons. It has more irregularities than ionization energy; AP questions focus on the general trend and on Coulombic reasoning.

4. Trend explanations on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the structural difference first: "Na and Mg are in the same period" or "Na and K are in the same group."
  • Same period: cite the greater number of protons (nuclear charge) with valence electrons in the same shell.
  • Same group: cite the higher shell (greater distance) and more core electrons shielding the valence electrons.
  • Connect to Coulomb's law: greater attraction between the nucleus and the valence electrons → smaller radius, higher ionization energy.
  • For the N/O and Be/B exceptions, name the subshell and the cause: electron pairing repulsion in 2p⁴, or a 2p electron that is higher in energy than 2s.
  • For successive ionization energies, point to the large jump and say it means removing a core electron.

Common mistakes

  • Explaining with "it wants a full octet" or "it is more stable." These earn no credit; use charge and distance.
  • Saying shielding increases across a period. Core shielding stays about the same; the nuclear charge grows.
  • Saying a larger atom has more protons, so it holds electrons more strongly. Distance wins down a group.
  • Comparing elements that differ in both period and group without checking whether both trends point the same way.
  • Forgetting that cations are smaller and anions larger than their neutral atoms.
  • Mixing up ionization energy (removing an electron) with electron affinity (adding one).
