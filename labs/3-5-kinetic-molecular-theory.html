2. Key ideas

The model

Kinetic molecular theory

An ideal gas is modeled as particles in constant, random, straight-line motion. Their own volume is negligible compared with the container, they neither attract nor repel each other, and their collisions are elastic: kinetic energy is exchanged but not lost.

KEavg ∝ T

Temperature is average kinetic energy

The average kinetic energy of the particles is directly proportional to the kelvin temperature. Double the kelvin temperature and you double the average KE. At the same temperature, every gas has the same average KE, whatever its molar mass.

KE = ½mv²

Heavier means slower

If two gases have the same average KE, the one with more mass per particle must move more slowly. Average speed is proportional to √(T/M), so a gas with 4 times the molar mass moves at half the average speed at the same temperature.

Curve shape

Reading the distribution

Higher temperature or lower molar mass shifts the peak to higher speed and makes the curve lower and broader. The area stays the same because the number of molecules does not change; the curve spreads out rather than growing.

Not one speed

A range of speeds

Collisions constantly trade energy between particles, so at any moment some are nearly still and some are very fast. Temperature tells you the average; the distribution tells you the spread. The most probable speed (the peak) is a little less than the average speed.

Link to kinetics

The fast tail

Raising the temperature greatly increases the fraction of particles in the high-speed tail. This is why a small temperature rise can speed up a reaction a lot: many more collisions have enough energy to react (topic 5.5).

4. KMT on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Connect temperature to average kinetic energy: "the higher-temperature sample has greater average KE."
  • At the same temperature, say the average KEs are equal, then use KE = ½mv² to argue the lighter particles must have a greater average speed.
  • When you sketch a second curve for a higher temperature: peak shifted right, lower peak, broader curve, and roughly the same area. Label the curves.
  • Refer to the fraction (or number) of particles with speed or energy above a threshold when linking to reaction rate.
  • Use molar masses from the periodic table to justify speed rankings: compare M, not the number of atoms.
  • Name the KMT assumption that applies (negligible volume, no attractions, elastic collisions) when a question asks why the ideal gas law works.

Common mistakes

  • Saying heavier gases have more kinetic energy. At the same temperature, average KE is the same for every gas.
  • Drawing the hotter curve taller. The higher-temperature curve is lower and broader, with the same area.
  • Saying all molecules in a sample move at the same speed. There is always a distribution.
  • Saying temperature is proportional to speed. Average KE is proportional to T; average speed is proportional to √T.
  • Using Celsius. Doubling from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double the average KE.
  • Mixing up "more particles" with "faster particles." Adding gas at the same T does not change the speed distribution.
