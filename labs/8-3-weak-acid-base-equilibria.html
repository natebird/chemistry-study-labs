2. Key ideas

Partial ionization

Most weak acid stays as HA

HA(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq) reaches equilibrium with only a small fraction ionized. So [H₃O⁺] is much less than the starting acid concentration, and a weak acid has a higher pH than a strong acid at the same concentration.

The constant

Ka and pKa

Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA], with water left out because it is the solvent. A larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger weak acid. Ka depends on temperature but not on concentration.

ICE table

Solve for x

Start with C of HA and essentially no ions. Let x ionize: x = [H₃O⁺] = [A⁻] and [HA] = C − x. Then Ka = x²/(C − x). When x is small next to C, C − x ≈ C and x ≈ √(KaC). Check that x/C is under 5%; if not, solve the quadratic.

Dilution

Percent ionization rises as C falls

Percent ionization = x/C × 100% ≈ √(Ka/C) × 100%. Diluting a weak acid 100-fold raises its percent ionization about 10-fold, even though [H₃O⁺] goes down. Le Châtelier explains it: dilution favors the side with more dissolved particles.

Weak bases

Same math with Kb

B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq), Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]. The ICE table gives x = [OH⁻], so find pOH first, then pH = 14.00 − pOH. Ammonia and amines are the usual examples.

Conjugate pairs

Ka × Kb = Kw

For an acid and its conjugate base, Ka × Kb = 1.0 × 10−14 and pKa + pKb = 14.00 at 25 °C. The weaker the acid, the stronger its conjugate base: CN⁻ (from very weak HCN) is a noticeably basic ion, while Cl⁻ (from HCl) is not basic at all.

4. Weak acids and bases on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the equilibrium with a double arrow and the Ka (or Kb) expression before substituting numbers.
  • Substitute equilibrium concentrations: Ka = x²/(C − x). State the assumption if you drop the x: "x is small compared with 0.10."
  • For a base, label x as [OH⁻], then convert: pOH = −log x and pH = 14.00 − pOH.
  • To find Ka from a pH: [H₃O⁺] = 10−pH = x, then Ka = x²/(C − x). This is a favorite reverse question.
  • Percent ionization = [H₃O⁺]eq/[HA]0 × 100%. Use it to justify "weak": a few percent or less.
  • For a conjugate base, find Kb = Kw/Ka first, and say which pair you mean.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a weak acid as strong: pH ≠ −log C. For 0.10 M acetic acid that gives 1.00 instead of 2.87.
  • Forgetting the square root: x = √(KaC), not KaC.
  • Reporting −log x as the pH of a weak base. That number is the pOH.
  • Thinking dilution lowers percent ionization. It raises it; it is [H₃O⁺] that drops.
  • Using C − x ≈ C when x is more than about 5% of C (for example, 0.010 M HF), which overestimates [H₃O⁺].
  • Saying a larger Ka means a larger pH. A larger Ka means more H₃O⁺ and a lower pH at the same concentration.
