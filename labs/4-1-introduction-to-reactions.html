Heat, light, gas, a precipitate, or a color change can be evidence of a chemical change, but none of them proves it on its own.     2. Key ideas

    Same composition

    Physical change

    A physical change alters a substance's properties or form but not its composition. Phase changes (melting, boiling, sublimation) and making or separating mixtures are the common examples. The particles before and after are the same particles.

    New composition

    Chemical change

    A chemical change turns substances into new substances, usually with different compositions. Atoms are rearranged into new combinations: old bonds break and new bonds form. Nothing is created or destroyed, so atoms of every element are conserved.

    Possible evidence

    Signs of a chemical change

    Production of heat or light, formation of a gas, formation of a precipitate, and a color change are possible evidence of a chemical change. The CED says "possible": each sign also shows up in some physical changes, so the strongest case combines the observation with what the substances are.

    Evidence is not proof

    Check for a new substance

    Bubbles in boiling water are water vapor, not a new gas. Crystals that appear when a solution cools are the same solute coming out. Food coloring changes color by mixing. Ask: is the gas, solid, or color a different substance from what you started with?

    Conservation

    Why we balance

    Because atoms are only rearranged, a correct equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. That is also why mass is conserved: total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. Mass seems to "disappear" only when a gas escapes an open container.

    Coefficients only

    How to balance

    Change only the coefficients in front of formulas. Changing a subscript changes the substance (H₂O to H₂O₂ is a different compound). Use the smallest whole-number set. Coefficients count particles or moles, not grams.

    4. Answering the FRQ

    What earns points on the FRQ

    • Name the specific observation ("a yellow solid formed when the two clear solutions were mixed"), then tie it to a new substance ("PbI₂, which was not present in either solution").
    • When asked to justify "chemical," point to composition: new substances with different formulas form because atoms are rearranged.
    • When asked to justify "physical," say the particles keep their identity: the same molecules or ions are present before and after; only their arrangement or the attractions between them change.
    • Write equations with correct formulas first, then balance with whole-number coefficients. Include states (s), (l), (g), (aq) when the question asks for them.
    • Check your balancing atom by atom, and check total charge if ions appear.
    • Use conservation of mass to explain mass data: if the container is open and a gas forms, the measured mass falls by the mass of gas that escaped.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying bubbles always mean a reaction. Boiling makes bubbles of the same substance in a different phase.
    • Calling any temperature change proof of a reaction. Dissolving and phase changes also absorb or release energy.
    • Balancing by changing subscripts. That changes the identity of the substance.
    • Thinking the total number of molecules must be the same on both sides. Only atoms are conserved: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O has 3 molecules on the left and 2 on the right.
    • Leaving coefficients that share a common factor (4H₂ + 2O₂ → 4H₂O). It is balanced, but not in lowest terms.
    • Forgetting the diatomic elements: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ are written as diatomic molecules when they appear as elements.
