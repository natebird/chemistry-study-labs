Reaction Rates Study Lab

A reaction rate is how fast reactant concentrations fall and product concentrations rise, in molarity per second. The coefficients of the balanced equation fix how the rates of the different species compare, and conditions such as concentration, temperature, surface area, and catalysts decide how fast the whole reaction runs. Here you follow a reaction on a concentration–time graph, read rates off it as slopes, and change the conditions to see what speeds it up.

2. Key ideas

Definition

Rate is change per time

A reaction rate is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, usually in mol L−1 s−1 (M/s). Rates are reported as positive numbers: a reactant's rate of disappearance is −Δ[reactant]/Δt, and a product's rate of appearance is +Δ[product]/Δt.

Stoichiometry

Coefficients set relative rates

In 2N2O5 → 4NO2 + O2, NO2 appears twice as fast as N2O5 disappears, and O2 appears half as fast. Dividing each species' rate by its coefficient gives one number, the rate of the reaction.

Reading a graph

Average vs. instantaneous

The average rate over an interval is the slope of the secant line, Δ[A]/Δt between two points. The instantaneous rate at one moment is the slope of the tangent line there. The initial rate is the instantaneous rate at t = 0.

Why rates slow

Concentration matters

As reactant is used up, reactant particles become less crowded, collide less often, and the rate usually drops. That is why concentration–time curves for reactants get flatter over time. More concentrated reactants (or a gas compressed to higher pressure) react faster.

Conditions

Temperature, surface area, catalyst

Higher temperature speeds nearly every reaction. Grinding a solid exposes more particles at its surface, so more collisions happen per second. A catalyst provides a faster pathway and is not used up. The particle-level reasons come in topics 5.5 and 5.11.

In the lab

Measuring a rate

You never watch concentration directly. You follow something that tracks it over time: absorbance of a colored species (Beer's law, A = εbc), pressure of a gas mixture, volume of gas collected, mass lost as a gas escapes, or pH. Topic 5.2 turns those data into a rate law.

4. Reaction rates on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • For relative rates, write the ratio from the balanced equation and show it: "rate of NO2 formation = (4/2)(0.024 M/s) = 0.048 M/s."
  • For an average rate from a table, show Δ[ ]/Δt with both readings and units: (0.500 − 0.800) M ÷ (30 − 0) s.
  • Say which line you used: "the slope of the tangent at t = 50 s" for an instantaneous rate, "the slope between the two points" for an average rate.
  • When explaining a rate change, name the particles: "more Zn atoms are exposed, so H3O+ ions collide with zinc more often."
  • Connect the experimental change to concentration or collisions explicitly, not just "it reacts faster."
  • Give units on every rate (M/s or mol L−1 s−1).

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying by the ratio the wrong way. A species with a bigger coefficient changes faster, not slower.
  • Reporting a reactant's rate as negative. The rate of disappearance is positive; it is Δ[A]/Δt that is negative.
  • Using the average rate when the question asks for the rate at one instant.
  • Saying a reaction slows because "the rate constant decreases." At constant temperature k is fixed; the concentrations drop.
  • Thinking a larger volume of the same solution reacts faster. Rate depends on concentration, not on the amount.
  • Saying higher temperature works only by "more collisions." The main reason is that more collisions have enough energy (topic 5.5).
