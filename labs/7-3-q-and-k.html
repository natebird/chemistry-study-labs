Reaction Quotient and Equilibrium Constant Study Lab

Every reversible reaction has an expression built from its balanced equation: products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Fill it with the amounts present at any moment and you get the reaction quotient Q; fill it with equilibrium amounts and you get the equilibrium constant K. Build the expressions for six reactions, see which substances are left out and why, and watch Q move relative to K as you change the amounts.

2. Key ideas

Law of mass action

Products over reactants

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the expression is [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b). Coefficients become exponents. Multiply within the numerator and within the denominator; never add.

Same form

Q and K use one expression

Q uses the amounts present at any moment. K is the special value of Q when the system is at equilibrium. As a reaction proceeds, Q changes and tends toward K; at equilibrium Kc = Qc and Kp = Qp.

Left out

Pure solids and liquids

A pure solid or pure liquid has a concentration that does not depend on how much of it is present, so it does not appear in Q or K. Water as the solvent in an aqueous reaction is left out. Water as a gas, H2O(g), is included.

Two kinds

Kc and Kp

Kc uses molar concentrations, written [X]. Kp uses partial pressures of gases, written PX. Only gases can appear in Kp. For the same reaction they usually have different values, so check which one a question uses. Converting between them is not on the AP exam.

Comparing

Q tells you how far from equilibrium

If Q < K, there is too little product relative to reactant, so the reaction will proceed forward. If Q > K, it will proceed in reverse. If Q = K, the system is at equilibrium. (Topic 7.10 uses this to predict shifts.)

Units

K is written without units

K and Q are normally reported as plain numbers, and AP questions give them that way. Use mol/L for Kc and, normally, atm for Kp when you substitute.

4. Q and K on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the expression exactly as asked: Kc with square brackets, Kp with partial pressures. An expression for the wrong one usually earns no credit.
  • Put products in the numerator and reactants in the denominator, with coefficients as exponents.
  • Leave out solids, pure liquids, and solvent water; keep every (g) and (aq) species.
  • When asked for Q, show the substitution with the given values, then the number.
  • Justify a direction by comparing Q with K in words: "Q < K, so the reaction proceeds to the right (toward products)."
  • Report K and Q without units.

Common mistakes

  • Writing reactants over products.
  • Multiplying a concentration by its coefficient (2[NH3]) instead of raising it to that power ([NH3]2).
  • Including a solid or H2O(l) in the expression, or leaving out H2O(g).
  • Putting aqueous ions into a Kp expression; only gases have partial pressures.
  • Using initial amounts in K. K uses equilibrium amounts; anything else gives Q.
  • Adding terms in the denominator ([N2] + [H2]3) instead of multiplying them.
