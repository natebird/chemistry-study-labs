2. Key ideas

PV = nRT

One equation, four variables

For an ideal gas, pressure times volume equals moles times R times kelvin temperature. With R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), P must be in atm, V in liters, n in moles, and T in kelvins. If you know any three, you can find the fourth.

Kelvin only

Temperature must be absolute

Gas laws are proportional to kelvin temperature, because 0 K is where particle motion (and pressure) would bottom out. Convert with K = °C + 273. Going from 25 °C to 50 °C raises T from 298 K to 323 K, a factor of 1.08, not 2.

Particle view

Where pressure comes from

Pressure is the force per area from particles hitting the walls. More particles (n up) or a smaller box (V down) means more frequent hits. Higher temperature means faster particles that hit both more often and harder.

Proportional reasoning

Hold some variables constant

Rearrange to P1V1/(n1T1) = P2V2/(n2T2) and cancel what does not change. Constant n and T: P and V are inversely proportional. Constant n and P: V is directly proportional to T. Constant T and P: V is directly proportional to n.

Dalton's law

Partial pressures add

In a mixture each gas exerts the pressure it would exert alone in the same container: PA = nART/V. The total is the sum. The partial pressure is also the mole fraction times the total: PA = XAPtotal, with XA = nA/ntotal.

Identity doesn't matter

Moles count, not mass

At the same T and V, 1 mol of helium and 1 mol of nitrogen exert the same pressure. Heavier particles move slower but hit harder, and the two effects cancel. That is why 1 mol of any ideal gas fills 22.4 L at 273 K and 1 atm.

4. Gas laws on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Write the equation first (PV = nRT or PA = XAPtotal), then substitute numbers with units, then give the answer with units.
  • Convert first: °C to K (add 273), torr or mm Hg to atm (divide by 760), mL to L (divide by 1000), grams to moles (divide by molar mass).
  • Match R to your units. Use 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) for pressure–volume problems and 8.314 J/(mol·K) only when energy in joules is involved.
  • For "what happens to P if…" questions, name what is constant and give the proportional reasoning ("V is halved at constant n and T, so P doubles").
  • Explain with particles: more frequent and/or more forceful collisions with the container walls.
  • Report three significant figures unless the data say otherwise; graders accept answers within rounding.

Common mistakes

  • Using °C in PV = nRT. A negative or zero Celsius temperature gives nonsense; a doubled Celsius temperature is not a doubled kelvin temperature.
  • Mixing units: pressure in torr with R in L·atm/(mol·K), or volume in mL.
  • Using mass instead of moles. PV = nRT counts particles; 4 g of He and 28 g of N₂ are both 1 mol and give the same pressure.
  • Thinking a heavier gas exerts more pressure at the same n, V, T. It does not; the slower speed cancels the larger mass.
  • Finding a partial pressure with the wrong mole fraction, such as nA/nB instead of nA/ntotal.
  • Saying adding a second gas to a rigid tank changes the first gas's partial pressure. It does not; only the total changes.
