Enthalpy of Formation Study Lab

A standard enthalpy of formation, ΔH°f, is the enthalpy change to make one mole of a substance from its elements in their standard states. With a table of ΔH°f values you can find ΔH° for almost any reaction: products minus reactants, each times its coefficient. Below, every reaction is routed through its elements so you can see why that works.

1. Down to the elements and back

Elements in their standard states sit at zero. Taking the reactants apart into elements is the reverse of forming them (−ΣΔH°f of reactants); building the products from those elements is +ΣΔH°f of products. The two legs add to ΔH°rxn. Values are standard textbook ΔH°f data at 25 °C.

2. Key ideas

Definition

One mole, from elements, in standard states

ΔH°f is the ΔH for a formation reaction: exactly 1 mol of product made from its elements in their most stable forms at 1 atm and 25 °C. For water: H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l), ΔH°f = −285.8 kJ/mol. The ½ is allowed because the product must be 1 mol.

Zero

Elements in standard states are zero

Forming O₂(g) from O₂(g) is no reaction at all, so ΔH°f = 0. The same is true for Fe(s), C(graphite), H₂(g), N₂(g), Br₂(l). But O₃(g), Br₂(g), and C(diamond) are not the standard forms, so their ΔH°f values are not zero.

The equation

Products minus reactants

ΔH°rxn = ΣnΔH°f(products) − ΣmΔH°f(reactants), where n and m are coefficients. This equation is on the AP equations sheet. It is the opposite order from the bond-enthalpy method (broken − formed).

Why it works

A path through the elements

Any reaction can be rewritten as "take the reactants apart into elements, then build the products." Reversing a formation reaction flips its sign, so the first leg is −ΣΔH°f(reactants). Adding the legs is Hess's law (topic 6.9).

Sign of ΔH°f

What a negative value means

A negative ΔH°f (CO₂, −393.5) means the compound is lower in enthalpy than its elements: forming it releases heat. A positive value (NO, +90.3) means it is higher in enthalpy, so forming it from its elements absorbs heat.

Phases matter

H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) are different

ΔH°f of H₂O(l) is −285.8 kJ/mol; of H₂O(g), −241.8. The 44.0 kJ difference is the enthalpy of vaporization at 25 °C (at 100 °C it is 40.7 kJ/mol, the value used in topic 6.5). The method works for physical processes too: H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) has ΔH° = −241.8 − (−285.8) = +44.0 kJ.

4. Enthalpies of formation on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Write the equation from the equations sheet, then substitute with coefficients shown: ΔH° = [1(−393.5) + 2(−285.8)] − [1(−74.8) + 2(0)].
  • Write 0 for elements in their standard states so the grader sees you know it.
  • Match the phase in the equation to the phase in the table (H₂O(l) vs. H₂O(g)).
  • For a missing ΔH°f, set up the same equation with the given ΔH°rxn, then solve for the single unknown. Check its sign makes sense.
  • Give units: kJ/molrxn for ΔH°rxn. Then, if asked for heat for a given mass, continue with q = nrxn × ΔH (topic 6.6).
  • When writing a formation reaction, make exactly 1 mol of product and use fractions for the elements if needed.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to multiply each ΔH°f by its coefficient.
  • Subtracting reactants − products, which flips the sign.
  • Using the wrong phase of water or another substance.
  • Assigning a nonzero value to an element in its standard state, or zero to a compound because it isn't in the table.
  • Writing a formation reaction that makes 2 mol of product, or that starts from atoms or compounds instead of elements in standard states.
  • Dropping a negative sign when substituting a negative ΔH°f into "− Σ reactants." Use brackets.
