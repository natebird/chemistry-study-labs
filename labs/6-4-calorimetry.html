Heat Capacity and Calorimetry Study Lab

You cannot measure the energy a reaction releases directly, but you can measure what it does to a known mass of water. Calorimetry turns a temperature change into an amount of energy with q = mcΔT, then uses conservation of energy, qprocess = −qwater, to find a specific heat or an enthalpy change. 2. Key ideas

The equation

q = mcΔT

The energy to change the temperature of a sample is its mass times its specific heat times the temperature change, ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial. A positive q means the sample gained energy; negative means it lost energy. With moles instead of grams, q = nCmΔT, using the molar heat capacity.

Same energy, different ΔT

Specific heat capacity

Specific heat is the energy needed to warm 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. Water's, 4.18 J/(g·°C), is unusually large, so the same energy changes the temperature of water far less than an equal mass of metal. Iron warms about 9 times as much as water for the same energy per gram.

First law

qprocess = −qwater

Energy is conserved. In an insulated calorimeter, whatever the reaction, dissolving salt, or hot metal loses, the water gains, and the reverse. Measure the water, flip the sign, and you have the process.

Reading the thermometer

Direction from the temperature

If the mixture's temperature rises, the dissolving or reacting system released energy: exothermic, ΔH < 0. If it falls, the system absorbed energy: endothermic, ΔH > 0.

Per mole

From q to ΔH

ΔH = qprocess ÷ moles of the limiting reactant (or of solute dissolved). Convert J to kJ. For a neutralization, the moles of water formed come from the limiting reactant, not from the total of both solutions.

Error analysis

Heat lost to the room

A real cup exchanges some energy with the room, so the measured |ΔT| is too small. Then |qwater| is too small, and the calculated |ΔH| or specific heat comes out too small too.

4. Calorimetry on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write q = mcΔT with numbers and units substituted: "q = (52.0 g)(4.18 J/(g·°C))(10.2 °C) = 2.22 × 103 J."
  • Use the total mass of the solution (water plus solute, or both solutions combined) when the problem says to treat the solution as water.
  • State the conservation step explicitly with signs: qrxn = −qsoln.
  • Divide by moles of the limiting reactant (or of solute) and convert J to kJ for ΔH in kJ/mol. Give the sign.
  • Connect the sign to the observation: the temperature increased, so the process is exothermic and ΔH is negative.
  • For error questions, trace the effect: heat lost → |ΔT| too small → |q| too small → |ΔH| (or c) too small.

Common mistakes

  • Plugging in a temperature instead of a temperature change. ΔT is Tfinal − Tinitial.
  • Forgetting to flip the sign: qwater is positive when the reaction is exothermic.
  • Using only the water's mass and leaving out the dissolved solid or the second solution.
  • Dividing by the total moles of acid plus base instead of the moles of the limiting reactant.
  • Mixing grams with molar heat capacity, or moles with specific heat. Match the units.
  • Thinking a larger volume of the same solutions gives a larger ΔT. Doubling both amounts doubles q and the mass, so ΔT stays the same.
