2. Key ideas

The pull on electrons

Electronegativity

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. It increases across a period (more protons, same shielding, so a larger effective nuclear charge) and decreases down a group (valence electrons are farther from the nucleus). F is the highest; the alkali metals at the bottom left are the lowest.

Metal + nonmetal

Ionic bonds

A metal with a low ionization energy gives up its valence electrons to a nonmetal with a high electronegativity. The resulting cations and anions are held together by Coulombic attraction between opposite charges. No discrete molecules form, only a lattice of ions.

Nonmetal + nonmetal, unequal

Polar covalent bonds

Two nonmetals share a pair of electrons, but the more electronegative atom pulls the pair closer. It carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other atom a partial positive charge (δ+). The bigger the electronegativity difference, the more polar the bond.

Nonmetal + nonmetal, equal

Nonpolar covalent bonds

Identical atoms (H–H, Cl–Cl) share electrons exactly equally. Atoms with very similar electronegativities share almost equally: the CED treats C–H bonds as effectively nonpolar even though C is slightly more electronegative than H.

Metal + metal

Metallic bonds

Metal atoms release their valence electrons into a "sea" of delocalized electrons shared by the whole sample. The positive metal cores sit in a lattice held together by their attraction to this sea. Mobile electrons explain conductivity and malleability (topic 2.4).

A spectrum, not boxes

Ionic character is a continuum

Every real bond lies somewhere between perfectly equal sharing and complete transfer. Electronegativity difference is not the only factor: the AP exam classifies by element type (metal or nonmetal), and the best evidence for the bond type is a compound's measured properties.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the bond type and justify it with the kinds of elements: "Na is a metal and Cl is a nonmetal, so the bond is ionic."
  • Explain electronegativity with structure: "O has more protons than N in the same shell, so O attracts the bonding electrons more strongly."
  • For a polar bond, say which atom carries the partial negative charge and why: "Cl is more electronegative than H, so the shared electrons are closer to Cl, giving Cl a partial negative charge."
  • Compare polarity with the electronegativity difference, not with one atom's value alone.
  • For ionic bonds, connect to ionization energy: the metal's low first ionization energy makes it easy to lose electrons.
  • For metallic bonding, use the words "delocalized" or "sea of electrons" and mention the positive metal cations.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a bond "polar" because one atom has a high electronegativity. H–H and F–F are nonpolar: equal atoms share equally.
  • Putting δ− on the less electronegative atom. Electrons are pulled toward the more electronegative atom, so that is where δ− goes.
  • Saying ionic compounds "share" electrons, or describing NaCl as NaCl molecules. Ionic solids are lattices of ions.
  • Treating the ΔEN cutoffs as exact laws. They are guidelines; element type and measured properties decide.
  • Saying electronegativity increases down a group. It decreases, because the valence electrons are farther from the nucleus.
  • Quoting a memorized electronegativity value as the whole justification. Explain the trend in terms of nuclear charge and distance.
