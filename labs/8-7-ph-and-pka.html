pH and pKa Study Lab

Whether a weak acid exists mostly as HA or mostly as A− depends on one comparison: the pH of the solution against the pKa of the acid. Below the pKa, the protonated form wins; above it, the deprotonated form wins. 2. Key ideas

The rule

Compare pH with pKa

If pH < pKa, [HA] > [A−]: the protonated form predominates. If pH > pKa, [A−] > [HA]. If pH = pKa, the two are equal. This comparison is how the CED asks you to predict the predominant form.

Why it works

Ka fixes the ratio

Ka = [H3O+][A−]/[HA], so [A−]/[HA] = Ka/[H3O+]. When [H3O+] is larger than Ka (pH below pKa), the ratio is less than 1. Each pH unit changes the ratio tenfold.

Le Châtelier view

Lots of H3O+ pushes protons on

In HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−, a low pH means a high [H3O+], which drives A− back to HA. A high pH means OH− removes protons, which favors A−.

Weak bases too

Use the conjugate acid's pKa

For NH3, compare the pH with the pKa of NH4+ (9.26). Below 9.26, NH4+ predominates; above it, NH3 does. Get pKa from pKb with pKa + pKb = 14.

Indicators

A weak acid with two colors

An indicator HIn has one color and In− another. Its color tells you which form predominates, so the color change happens near pH = pKa of the indicator, over roughly two pH units.

Titrations

Match pKa to the equivalence pH

Choose an indicator whose pKa is close to the pH at the equivalence point. Then the color change (end point) happens at the equivalence point. Weak acid with strong base: equivalence pH > 7. Weak base with strong acid: < 7.

4. pH and pKa on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • State both numbers and the comparison: "pH 7.40 is greater than the pKa of 4.74, so [CH3COO−] > [CH3COOH]."
  • Name the species with formulas, not just "the acid form."
  • For a weak base, convert first: pKa(BH+) = 14.00 − pKb(B), then compare.
  • For indicator choice, give the equivalence-point pH and say the indicator's pKa is close to it.
  • To explain a color, say which form of the indicator predominates at that pH and what color that form has.
  • If asked to read pKa from a titration curve, it equals the pH at half the equivalence volume.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking "acidic solution means HA predominates." HCOOH (pKa 3.75) at pH 4.5 is mostly HCOO−, even though the solution is acidic.
  • Comparing the pH with 7 instead of with the pKa.
  • Using pKb of NH3 (4.74) as if it were the pKa of NH4+.
  • Saying the indicator should change color "at pH 7" for every titration. Only strong–strong titrations have an equivalence pH of 7.
  • Saying that at pH = pKa the acid is "half strong" or "neutral." It means [HA] = [A−], nothing more.
  • Forgetting the direction: higher pH (fewer H3O+) means more of the deprotonated form.
