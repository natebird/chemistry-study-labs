Pre-Equilibrium Approximation Study Lab

When a fast, reversible step comes before the slow step, the slow step's rate law contains an intermediate, and a measured rate law cannot. The fix is to treat the fast first step as being at equilibrium, solve for the intermediate, and substitute. Walk through that derivation for five classic mechanisms, then speed up the slow step to see when the approximation is trustworthy.

    2. Key ideas

    The problem

    The slow step has an intermediate

    If the slow step is not the first step, its rate law usually includes an intermediate made in an earlier step. Its concentration cannot be set or measured like a reactant's, so it cannot stay in the final rate law.

    The assumption

    Fast step at equilibrium

    If step 1 is fast and reversible and step 2 is slow, step 1 runs forward and backward many times before step 2 removes much intermediate. So step 1's forward and reverse rates are taken as equal.

    The algebra

    Set rates equal, solve, substitute

    Write k1[reactants] = k−1[products of step 1], solve for the intermediate, and substitute into the slow step's rate law. The constants combine: k = k2k1/k−1.

    Equilibrium link

    k1/k−1 = K1

    The ratio of the forward and reverse rate constants of the fast step is that step's equilibrium constant. This is also why the equilibrium expression for an elementary step looks like its rate laws (topic 7.1).

    Surprises

    Products and odd orders

    The final rate law can contain a product with a negative order, as in rate = k[O3]2[O2]−1 for ozone, because O2 drives step 1 backward and lowers the intermediate.

    When it fails

    Step 2 must be slow

    If step 2 uses the intermediate faster than step 1 can reverse, step 1 is no longer at equilibrium, and the rate approaches the rate of step 1 itself (the topic 5.8 case). The graph shows the switch.

    4. Pre-equilibrium on the FRQ

    What earns points on the FRQ

    • Start from the slow step's rate law and name the intermediate in it.
    • State the assumption: "Because step 1 is fast and reversible, its forward and reverse rates are equal."
    • Write that equality with rate constants, k1[NO]2 = k−1[N2O2], and solve for the intermediate.
    • Substitute and collect constants into one observed k. Show the final rate law contains only species whose concentrations can be measured.
    • Compare the result with the experimental rate law and say whether the mechanism is consistent.
    • Check that the steps add up to the overall equation.

    Common mistakes

    • Leaving the intermediate in the final rate law.
    • Writing the rate law from the fast step because it comes first.
    • Inverting the equilibrium ratio: K1 = k1/k−1, not k−1/k1. For 2NO ⇌ N2O2, [N2O2] = (k1/k−1)[NO]2.
    • Dropping a product that is also in step 1 (like O2 in the ozone mechanism); it belongs in the denominator.
    • Forgetting the exponent when the intermediate's coefficient is 2: from I2 ⇌ 2I, [I]2 = K[I2].
    • Using the overall coefficients as orders. The pre-equilibrium result often matches them by coincidence (2NO + O2), but not always (2O3 → 3O2).
