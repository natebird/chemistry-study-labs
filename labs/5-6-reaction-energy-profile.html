Reaction Energy Profile Study Lab

In an elementary reaction, old bonds stretch and break while new ones form. On the way, the atoms pass through a high-energy arrangement called the transition state, and the energy needed to reach it is the activation energy. Shape an energy profile, walk the atoms over the barrier, and change the temperature to see why only some collisions succeed.

2. Key ideas

Bonds

Break some, form some

An elementary reaction happens in one collision. During it, some bonds in the reactants break and new bonds in the products form. Breaking bonds costs energy before the new bonds pay it back, which is why there is a barrier at all.

The x-axis

Reaction coordinate

The horizontal axis tracks how far the atoms have rearranged from reactants to products. It is not time and it is not concentration. The vertical axis is potential energy.

The peak

Transition state

The highest point on the path for a step. Old bonds are partly broken and new bonds partly formed. It exists for an instant and cannot be isolated, which is what separates it from an intermediate (topic 5.7).

Two barriers

Forward and reverse Ea

Ea(forward) is measured from the reactants up to the transition state; Ea(reverse) from the products up to the same peak. They are linked: Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH. For an exothermic reaction the reverse barrier is the larger one.

Temperature

More collisions clear the bar

Heating does not lower Ea. It shifts the spread of collision energies so a larger fraction of collisions reach the transition state. Particles also collide a little more often, but the energy effect is the main reason the rate rises.

Arrhenius equation

The math behind it

The Arrhenius equation, k = Ae−Ea/RT, ties the rate constant to Ea and T: a higher barrier means a smaller k, and a higher T means a larger k. The CED states that calculations with it are not assessed, and it is not on the equations sheet. Know the relationship in words.

4. Energy profiles on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • When you draw a profile, label the axes (potential energy vs. reaction coordinate), put the products at the correct height relative to the reactants, and draw one smooth peak for one elementary step.
  • Mark Ea with an arrow from the reactant level to the top of the peak, and ΔH with an arrow from reactants to products.
  • For temperature questions, say the fraction (or number) of collisions with energy greater than or equal to Ea increases. Mention the energy distribution, not just "particles move faster."
  • Use the relationship Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH, and show the subtraction with signs.
  • Connect the barrier to bonds: energy is needed to begin breaking reactant bonds before product bonds form.
  • State that the transition state is the highest-energy arrangement along the reaction coordinate.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring Ea from the bottom of the graph or from the products instead of from the reactants.
  • Saying a higher temperature lowers the activation energy. Ea stays the same; only catalysts change the path (topic 5.11).
  • Thinking a very exothermic reaction must be fast. ΔH and Ea are independent; a reaction can release a lot of energy and still be slow.
  • Getting the sign of ΔH backward: products lower than reactants means ΔH is negative.
  • Calling the transition state an intermediate. An intermediate sits in a valley and can sometimes be detected; a transition state sits on a peak.
  • Reading the x-axis as time.
