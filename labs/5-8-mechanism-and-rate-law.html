Reaction Mechanism and Rate Law Study Lab

A reaction can only go as fast as its slowest step. When the first step of a mechanism is the slow one, the rate law of the whole reaction is just the rate law of that step, written from its coefficients. Run initial-rate experiments, find the experimental rate law, and test which proposed mechanism predicts it.

2. Key ideas

Elementary steps

Coefficients become orders

For an elementary step only, the rate law comes straight from the equation: A + B → products has rate = k[A][B]; 2A → products has rate = k[A]2. This works because the step happens in one collision of exactly those particles.

Overall reactions

Not from the overall equation

An overall equation is usually not elementary, so its coefficients do not give the rate law. The rate law must come from experiment or from a mechanism.

Bottleneck

Rate-determining step

The slowest step limits the overall rate, like the slowest station on an assembly line. Fast steps after it simply use up what it makes as soon as it is made.

The rule for 5.8

Slow first step: copy it

When the first step is the slow one (or every step is irreversible and the slow step involves only reactants), the overall rate law is the slow step's rate law. Species that react only in later fast steps do not appear in it.

Judging a mechanism

Two tests

A mechanism is plausible if (1) its steps add up to the overall balanced equation and (2) its predicted rate law matches the experimental one. Passing both makes it consistent with the evidence, not proven; another mechanism might also pass.

Coming next

Slow step with an intermediate

If the slow step is not first, its rate law usually contains an intermediate, which cannot appear in a measured rate law. Topic 5.9 shows how the pre-equilibrium approximation replaces it.

4. Mechanisms and rate laws on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the rate law of the slow step and say why it applies: "Step 1 is the rate-determining step, so the rate law is the rate law of step 1."
  • Compare orders species by species with the experimental rate law: "The mechanism predicts second order in NO2 and zero order in CO, which matches the data."
  • When choosing between mechanisms, give the reason the rejected one fails (its slow step gives the wrong orders, or its steps do not add up).
  • From initial-rate data, change one concentration at a time: if doubling [X] doubles the rate, first order; quadruples, second order; no change, zero order.
  • Report k with units that match the overall order: M−1 s−1 for second order, M−2 s−1 for third order, s−1 for first order.
  • Use the language "consistent with" rather than "proves."

Common mistakes

  • Writing the rate law from the overall equation's coefficients. 2NO2 + F2 → 2NO2F is first order in NO2, not second.
  • Including a reactant that only appears in a fast step after the slow step.
  • Picking the fast step as rate-determining because it "does more."
  • Forgetting to check that the steps add up to the overall equation before comparing rate laws.
  • Thinking a zero-order reactant is not needed. It is consumed; its concentration just does not affect the rate.
  • Writing a rate law that contains an intermediate. Measured rate laws contain only species whose concentrations you can set (topic 5.9 handles that case).
