2. Key ideas

Variable composition

Mixtures vs. pure substances

A pure substance (element or compound) has a fixed composition. A mixture is two or more substances physically combined, so its proportions can vary from sample to sample: salt water can be a little salty or very salty.

Elemental analysis

What the data measure

An elemental analysis reports the mass of an element in a sample. In a mixture, that element may come from one substance or from several. Knowing each substance's own percent of that element lets you work out the mixture.

Between the pure values

The weighted-average idea

The percent of an element in a mixture of A and B is a weighted average: %X = w(%X in A) + (1 − w)(%X in B), where w is the mass fraction of A. It always lies between the values for pure A and pure B.

Only one source

Purity of a sample

If only the compound contains the element (calcium in limestone with sand), then purity = measured %X ÷ %X in the pure compound. A sample that is 32.0% Ca is 32.0 ÷ 40.04 = 79.9% CaCO₃.

Mass % ≠ particle %

Count vs. mass

A 50:50 mixture by mass of NaCl and KCl has more NaCl formula units, because each NaCl unit is lighter. To compare numbers of particles, convert each mass to moles.

Same recipe, different ratio

Why it matters

Two samples with different percent compositions cannot both be the same pure compound. A percent composition that does not match the formula is evidence of an impurity or a mixture.

4. Mixtures on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Start by computing the percent of the element in each pure substance from its formula, and show the molar masses.
  • State which substance(s) contain the measured element. If only one does, the element's mass tells you that substance's mass directly.
  • Set up the weighted average with a variable, and show the algebra when solving for the mass fraction.
  • Report a percent purity or percent by mass with units and three significant figures.
  • For a claim about purity, compare the measured value with the theoretical value and say whether they agree.
  • When asked about numbers of particles, convert masses to moles before comparing.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a mixture as if it had a fixed formula. A mixture has no single formula or molar mass.
  • Assuming 50% by mass means 50% of the particles. Lighter formula units are more numerous in the same mass.
  • Forgetting that both substances can contain the element (Cl in both NaCl and KCl), so the measured Cl is not all from one.
  • Dividing by the wrong percent: purity uses the element's percent in the pure compound, not 100%.
  • Getting a mixture answer outside the range of the pure values. A Cl percent above 60.66% cannot come from NaCl and KCl alone.
  • Mixing up the element's mass with the compound's mass when converting to moles.
