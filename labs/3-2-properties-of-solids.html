2. Key ideas

Ionic solids

Ions in a lattice

Cations and anions held by strong Coulombic attractions. High melting points, hard but brittle: shifting a layer lines up like charges, which repel. They conduct only when melted or dissolved, when the ions are free to move. Higher charges and smaller ions give higher melting points (MgO vs. NaCl).

Molecular solids

Molecules held by IMFs

Discrete molecules held to each other by intermolecular forces (dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding). Melting overcomes only these weak forces, so melting points are low and the solids are soft. No charged particles move, so they do not conduct in any phase.

Covalent network solids

One giant molecule

Atoms covalently bonded to neighbors in three dimensions (diamond, SiO2, Si). Melting means breaking covalent bonds, so melting points are extremely high and the solids are very hard. Most do not conduct. Graphite is the exception: its layers have delocalized electrons and slide past each other.

Metallic solids

Cations in an electron sea

Metal cations surrounded by delocalized valence electrons. The electrons move freely, so metals conduct as solids and liquids. Layers of cations can slide without breaking the bonding, so metals are malleable and ductile. Melting points vary widely (Na 98 °C, Cu 1,085 °C).

Conductivity

Something charged has to move

Conduction needs mobile charged particles. Metals and graphite have mobile electrons. Ionic compounds have mobile ions only when molten or dissolved. Molecular and most network solids have neither.

Melting point

What must be overcome

Molecular: intermolecular forces (low). Ionic: ionic bonds (high). Network: covalent bonds (very high). Metallic: metallic bonding (wide range). On the exam, always say which attraction is overcome.

4. Solids on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Name the particles and the attraction: "NaCl is made of Na+ and Cl− ions held by strong Coulombic attractions."
  • For conductivity, say what is mobile: "In molten NaCl the ions are free to move and carry charge; in the solid they are held in fixed positions."
  • For a melting-point comparison between molecular and network solids, contrast IMFs with covalent bonds: "melting SiO2 requires breaking covalent bonds; turning solid CO2 into a gas only overcomes dispersion forces."
  • For brittleness, describe the shift: like-charged ions are brought next to each other and repel.
  • For malleability, say the delocalized electrons keep holding the cations together as layers slide.
  • For comparisons of ionic solids, use Coulomb's law: larger charges and smaller ions mean stronger attraction and a higher melting point.

Common mistakes

  • Saying solid NaCl conducts because it "has ions." The ions must be free to move.
  • Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular solid melts. Only intermolecular forces are overcome.
  • Calling SiO2 a molecular solid because its formula looks like CO2. SiO2 is a network; CO2 is molecular.
  • Saying all metals have very high melting points. Sodium melts at 98 °C and mercury is a liquid.
  • Saying sugar water conducts because sugar dissolves. Dissolving molecules does not make ions.
  • Saying sodium "dissolves" in water. It reacts to form H2 and NaOH, a chemical change.
