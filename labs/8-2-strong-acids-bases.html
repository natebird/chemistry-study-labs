2. Key ideas

Complete ionization

Strong means "all the way"

A strong acid gives its proton to water with essentially no molecules left over: HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), written with a single arrow. A particle diagram of a strong acid shows only H₃O⁺ and the anion, never intact HA.

Memorize

The strong acids

HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, and H₂SO₄. Any other acid you meet in AP Chemistry (HF, HNO₂, CH₃COOH, HCN, …) is weak. HF is the classic trap: it is a hydrohalic acid but a weak one.

Strong bases

Group 1 and Group 2 hydroxides

LiOH, NaOH, KOH (one OH⁻ each) and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂ (two OH⁻ each) dissociate completely. So [OH⁻] equals the concentration of a Group 1 hydroxide and twice the concentration of a Group 2 hydroxide.

The calculation

Concentration → ion → pH

Acid: [H₃O⁺] = C, pH = −log C. Base: [OH⁻] = C or 2C, pOH = −log[OH⁻], pH = 14.00 − pOH. Going backward, undo each step: 10−pH gives [H₃O⁺], and dividing [OH⁻] by 2 gives the concentration of a Group 2 hydroxide.

Dilution

Tenfold dilution = one pH unit

Diluting a strong acid 10 times divides [H₃O⁺] by 10, so pH rises by exactly 1.00. Diluting a strong base 10 times lowers pH by 1.00. Dilution pushes pH toward 7 but never past it: no amount of water turns an acid basic.

Strong ≠ concentrated

Strength and concentration are different

"Strong" describes how completely the solute ionizes; "concentrated" describes how much is dissolved. A 1.0 × 10−4 M HCl solution is strong but dilute (pH 4.00). Ca(OH)₂ and Sr(OH)₂ are only slightly soluble, but what dissolves is fully dissociated, so they are still strong bases.

Simplification: H₂SO₄ is on the list of strong acids because its first proton ionizes completely. The second proton (from HSO₄⁻) ionizes only partly, so [H₃O⁺] in an H₂SO₄ solution falls between C and 2C. This lab leaves H₂SO₄ out of the pH calculator for that reason.

4. Strong acids and bases on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • State why the shortcut works: "HNO₃ is a strong acid, so it ionizes completely and [H₃O⁺] = [HNO₃]₀ = 0.025 M."
  • For a base, get [OH⁻] first, then pOH, then pH = 14.00 − pOH. Show all three lines.
  • For a Group 2 hydroxide, write the dissociation equation with the coefficient 2 so the grader sees where [OH⁻] = 2C comes from.
  • Report pH with as many decimal places as the concentration has significant figures (0.025 M → pH 1.60).
  • When asked for concentration from pH, show the inverse log as its own step: [OH⁻] = 10−pOH.
  • For dilution, use moles: n = M × V stays the same, so Cnew = n ÷ Vnew. A tenfold dilution changes pH by exactly 1.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the 2 for Ba(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, and Ca(OH)₂, which makes the pH 0.30 too low.
  • Reporting pOH as the pH of a base. A base must have pH above 7 at 25 °C.
  • Setting up an ICE table and Ka for a strong acid. There is no equilibrium to solve; the reaction goes to completion.
  • Calling HF a strong acid because the other hydrogen halides are.
  • Thinking that diluting an acid enough makes it basic. Very dilute acid approaches pH 7 from below.
  • Drawing intact HCl molecules in a particle diagram of hydrochloric acid.
