An elementary reaction happens in one event, exactly as written: one particle falls apart, or two (rarely three) particles collide. Because the equation describes the collision itself, its coefficients give the rate law directly. 2. Key ideas

Definition

One event, as written

An elementary reaction (elementary step) is a single molecular event: the particles on the left collide (or one particle breaks apart) and the particles on the right come out. Most overall reactions are made of several elementary steps, called a mechanism (topic 5.7).

The shortcut

Coefficients become exponents

For an elementary step only, the rate law comes from the stoichiometry of the collision: aA + bB → products has rate = k[A]a[B]b. The order in each reactant equals its coefficient.

Molecularity

How many particles collide

Unimolecular (one particle, first order): A → products. Bimolecular (two, second order): A + B or 2A. Termolecular (three, third order): rare, because three particles must meet at the same instant.

Why it works

Counting meetings

The chance that an A is at a given spot is proportional to [A]; the chance that an A and a B are there together is proportional to [A][B]. Two A's together: [A]2. So the collision frequency, and the rate, follow the coefficients.

Trap

Overall equations are different

You cannot read a rate law from an overall balanced equation. 2NO2 + F2 → 2NO2F has rate = k[NO2][F2], found by experiment. Only if a problem says a step is elementary may you use its coefficients.

Rare events

Three at once

Elementary steps with three or more particles colliding at once are rare, and four-particle collisions are not considered realistic. An overall equation with many reactant particles, such as 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, is expected to happen in several steps (that one is in fact a chain of steps involving H, O, and OH).

4. Elementary reactions on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Say why you may use the coefficients: "Because this step is elementary, the rate law follows from its stoichiometry: rate = k[NO2]2."
  • Name the molecularity correctly: 2NO2 → NO3 + NO is bimolecular (two particles), even though only one kind of particle is involved.
  • Explain rare termolecular steps with particles: three particles must collide at the same instant, which is far less likely than two.
  • When a question gives only an overall equation, say the rate law must be determined experimentally.
  • Connect concentration to collision frequency: doubling [A] doubles how often A meets B.
  • Give the overall order as the sum of the coefficients of the reactants in the step.

Common mistakes

  • Using coefficients of an overall equation as orders.
  • Including products in the rate law of an elementary step. Only the colliding reactants appear.
  • Calling 2A → products "unimolecular" because it has one kind of reactant. Count particles, not kinds.
  • Writing rate = 2k[A] for 2A → products. The coefficient becomes an exponent: rate = k[A]2.
  • Thinking a step with four reactant particles could be elementary.
  • Mixing up molecularity (a property of an elementary step) with the measured order of an overall reaction.
