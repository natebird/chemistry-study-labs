The parts of a mixture can be separated because they are attracted to their surroundings by intermolecular forces of different strengths. In chromatography, a component that sticks more strongly to the stationary phase moves more slowly. In distillation, the component with weaker intermolecular forces boils first. 2. Key ideas

Two phases

How chromatography works

A mobile phase (the solvent) flows past a stationary phase (paper, silica, a packed column). Each component is attracted to both. The stronger its attraction to the stationary phase compared with the mobile phase, the more time it spends stuck and the shorter the distance it moves.

Polar stationary phase

Polar sticks, nonpolar runs

Paper and silica are covered in −OH groups. Components that can hydrogen-bond or have strong dipoles are held tightly and stay near the baseline. Nonpolar components, with only London dispersion forces, are carried along near the solvent front.

Rf

Retention factor

Rf = distance traveled by the component ÷ distance traveled by the solvent front, both measured from the baseline. It has no units and is between 0 and 1. A small Rf means strong attraction to the stationary phase. The Rf formula is not on the AP equations sheet, so know it.

Solvent choice

A more polar solvent moves everything

On a polar plate, a more polar mobile phase competes better for the components, so every Rf rises. The order of the spots does not change. Too polar and everything rides near the front; too nonpolar and everything stays near the baseline.

Column

Column chromatography

The same idea in a tube. Solvent carries the mixture down through the stationary phase, and components come out (elute) in order: the one least attracted to the stationary phase first.

Distillation

Weaker IMFs boil first

When a liquid mixture is heated, the component with weaker intermolecular forces has the higher vapor pressure and lower boiling point, so its vapor reaches the condenser first. Separation works best when the boiling points are far apart.

4. Explaining it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the specific IMFs: "compound Z has an −OH group, so it forms hydrogen bonds with the −OH groups of the silica."
  • Compare attractions to both phases: the component more strongly attracted to the stationary phase travels a shorter distance.
  • Calculate Rf from the baseline, not from the bottom edge of the plate, and give it without units.
  • Identify an unknown by matching its Rf (and color) with a standard run on the same plate with the same solvent.
  • For distillation, connect weaker IMFs → higher vapor pressure → lower boiling point → collected first.
  • When comparing London dispersion forces, cite the number of electrons (polarizability) or the contact surface area.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a component moves farther because it is lighter. Mass is not the reason; attraction to the two phases is.
  • Measuring spot distances from the bottom of the paper instead of the pencil baseline.
  • Thinking a more polar solvent reverses the order of spots on a polar plate. It raises all Rf values.
  • Drawing the baseline in ink. Ink dissolves and runs as its own set of spots; use pencil.
  • Saying the component with stronger IMFs boils first. Stronger IMFs mean a higher boiling point.
  • Saying covalent bonds break in distillation. Only intermolecular attractions are overcome.
