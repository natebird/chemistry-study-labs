2. Key ideas

Count the peaks

One peak per step

Each elementary step passes through its own transition state, so a two-step mechanism has two peaks and a three-step mechanism has three. The number of peaks tells you the number of steps.

Count the valleys

Intermediates sit in valleys

Between two peaks the energy dips to a local minimum: the intermediate. It is more stable than the transition states on either side, so it lasts long enough that it can sometimes be detected.

Measure each step

Step activation energies

A step's Ea is measured from where that step starts (reactants or the intermediate valley before it) up to that step's own peak, not from the reactants.

Slowest step

The biggest step barrier

The step with the largest activation energy is the slowest and is rate-determining. It is not always the tallest peak on the graph, and it is not always the first step.

Overall energy change

ΔH from start to finish

Overall ΔH is the energy of the products minus the energy of the reactants. It equals the sum of the steps' ΔH values. Individual steps can be endothermic even when the overall reaction is exothermic.

Matching a mechanism

Profile ↔ steps

A fast step drawn first means a small first barrier; a slow second step means a large barrier out of the intermediate valley. When asked to sketch a profile for a mechanism, match peak count, relative barrier sizes, and the final ΔH.

4. Multistep profiles on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Draw exactly as many peaks as there are elementary steps, with a valley for each intermediate.
  • Make the slow step's barrier (measured from its starting level) visibly the largest.
  • Put the products at the correct height relative to the reactants for the overall ΔH, and label reactants, intermediates and products.
  • Justify the rate-determining step with the energy profile: "Step 2 has the larger activation energy, so it is slower."
  • For a fast first step that reaches equilibrium, draw a small first barrier so the intermediate can easily go back to reactants.
  • Connect the profile to the rate law: the slow step on the graph is the one whose rate law sets the overall rate law.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring every step's Ea from the reactants instead of from the start of that step.
  • Assuming the tallest peak is always the slow step. If an earlier step drops into a deep valley, the climb out of that valley can be the largest barrier even though its peak is lower.
  • Calling a valley a transition state, or a peak an intermediate.
  • Drawing one peak for a two-step mechanism.
  • Using a step's ΔH as the overall ΔH.
  • Thinking that the first step must be the slowest.
