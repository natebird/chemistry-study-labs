Cell Potential and Free Energy Study Lab

Every redox reaction can be split into a reduction and an oxidation, and a table of standard reduction potentials tells you how hard each half "pulls" on electrons. The difference is the standard cell potential, and ΔG° = −nFE° turns it into free energy: positive E° means a thermodynamically favored reaction. Choose half-reactions and follow the calculation.

2. Key ideas

Reading the table

Every E° is written as a reduction

A more positive E° means that species is more easily reduced. In a favored cell, the half-reaction higher on the table runs as written (reduction, cathode), and the lower one runs in reverse (oxidation, anode).

The formula

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Use both values exactly as they appear in the reduction table and subtract. (Equivalently, flip the sign of the anode's E° and add. Do one or the other, never both.)

Intensive

Never multiply E°

Balancing electrons may require multiplying a half-reaction by 2 or 3. That changes n, but not E°: a potential is energy per unit charge, so it does not depend on how much reaction you write.

Free energy

ΔG° = −nFE°

n is the moles of electrons transferred in the balanced equation and F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻. With E° in volts (J/C), ΔG° comes out in J per mole of reaction; divide by 1000 for kJ. Unlike E°, ΔG° does scale with the coefficients, through n.

Signs

Positive E°, negative ΔG°, K > 1

E°cell > 0 ⇔ ΔG° < 0 ⇔ K > 1: the reaction is thermodynamically favored and can run as a galvanic cell. E°cell < 0 means the reaction as written is unfavored and needs an external potential (an electrolytic cell).

Reverse it

Swapping flips every sign

Writing the reaction backward reverses which half is the cathode. E°cell and ΔG° change sign; K becomes 1/K. The magnitude stays the same.

Six steps for any cell

  1. Find both half-reactions in the table (written as reductions) and their E° values.
  2. Decide which is reduced: for a favored cell, the one with the more positive E°. Reverse the other one: it is the oxidation.
  3. Multiply the half-reactions so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained. Do not multiply E°.
  4. Add and cancel electrons to get the overall equation; n is the number of electrons that cancelled.
  5. E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode.
  6. ΔG° = −nFE°cell; convert J to kJ. Negative ΔG° (positive E°) means thermodynamically favored.

4. Cell potential on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the setup with values: "E°cell = 0.80 V − (−0.76 V) = 1.56 V."
  • State n with a reason: "2 mol e⁻ are transferred per mole of reaction as written."
  • Show ΔG° = −nFE° with units: −(2 mol e⁻)(96,485 C/mol e⁻)(1.56 J/C) = −3.01 × 105 J/molrxn = −301 kJ/molrxn.
  • Justify "favored" with the sign: "E°cell > 0, so ΔG° < 0 and the reaction is thermodynamically favored."
  • When asked which species is reduced, point to the more positive reduction potential.
  • Report E° to the hundredths place to match the table, and ΔG° to three significant figures.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying E° by the coefficient used to balance electrons (2 × 0.80 V). E° is intensive.
  • Flipping the sign of the anode's E° and also subtracting it, which double-counts the reversal.
  • Using the wrong n: it is the total electrons transferred in the balanced equation (6 for 2Al + 3Ni2+), not the charge on one ion.
  • Forgetting that volts are J/C, then leaving ΔG° in J while calling it kJ (off by 1000).
  • Dropping the minus sign in ΔG° = −nFE°, so a favored cell appears to have ΔG° > 0.
  • Thinking a larger E° means a faster reaction. E° tells you about thermodynamics, not rate.
