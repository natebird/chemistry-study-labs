2. Key ideas

Particle view

Temperature reflects average kinetic energy

The particles in a warmer body have a greater average kinetic energy than those in a cooler body. Individual particles have a spread of energies, so some particles in the cold body move faster than some in the hot body.

Mechanism

Collisions carry the energy

Where two bodies touch, their particles collide. A single collision can move energy either way, but on average the more energetic particles lose energy and the less energetic ones gain it. The net result is energy flowing from hot to cold: heat transfer.

End state

Thermal equilibrium

Energy keeps flowing until the average kinetic energies, and so the temperatures, are equal. Particles still collide and still exchange energy at equilibrium, but the exchanges balance, so there is no net transfer.

Conservation

Energy lost = energy gained

In an isolated pair, qA + qB = 0. The hot body's q is negative and the cold body's q is positive, with equal sizes. This is the first law, and it is the basis of every calorimetry calculation in topic 6.4.

Who changes more

Heat capacity sets the ΔT

Both bodies move the same amount of energy, but the one with the smaller heat capacity (mass × specific heat) changes temperature more. A small metal sample in a lot of water swings a long way; the water barely moves.

Words matter

Heat is not temperature

Temperature is a property of a body. Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. Bodies do not "contain heat," and "cold" never flows: energy flows from the warmer body into the colder one.

Final temperature of two bodies (no phase change, no losses)

  1. Energy lost by the hot body = energy gained by the cold body: mAcA(Tf − TA) + mBcB(Tf − TB) = 0.
  2. Solve for Tf = (mAcATA + mBcBTB) ÷ (mAcA + mBcB): a weighted average of the starting temperatures.
  3. Check: Tf must lie between the two starting temperatures, and closer to the body with the larger m × c.

4. Heat transfer on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Use the particle model explicitly: "The particles of the metal have a greater average kinetic energy than the particles of the water."
  • Name the mechanism: "collisions between particles at the surface transfer energy from the metal to the water."
  • Describe the end state with both ideas: "until the average kinetic energies are equal, so the temperatures are equal (thermal equilibrium)."
  • For conservation, write it as an equation with signs: qmetal = −qwater.
  • When comparing temperature changes, cite heat capacity: the body with the smaller m × c has the larger ΔT for the same energy.
  • Say "average" kinetic energy. Individual particles in the same body have different energies.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "cold transfers to the hot object." Only energy moves, and it moves from hot to cold.
  • Saying collisions stop at equilibrium. They continue; only the net transfer stops.
  • Assuming the final temperature is the simple average of the two. That is true only when both bodies have the same m × c.
  • Assuming both bodies change temperature by the same amount. They transfer the same energy, not the same ΔT.
  • Claiming every particle in the hot body is faster than every particle in the cold body.
  • Mixing up heat and temperature: "the water has more heat" should be "the water has a higher temperature" or "more thermal energy."
