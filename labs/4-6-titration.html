Introduction to Titration Study Lab

In a titration, a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added from a burette to a measured volume of the solution being analyzed (the analyte) until the two have reacted in exactly the stoichiometric ratio. That moment is the equivalence point. Add titrant drop by drop, watch the particles and the indicator, and use the volume at the end point to calculate an unknown concentration.

2. Key ideas

Known vs. unknown

Titrant and analyte

The titrant is the solution of known concentration in the burette. The analyte is the solution being analyzed, measured into the flask with a pipet. The goal is to find the amount (and so the concentration) of the analyte.

Stoichiometric amounts

Equivalence point

The equivalence point is reached when the moles of titrant added and the moles of analyte present are in the mole ratio of the balanced equation. It is not "equal moles" unless the ratio is 1 : 1, and it is not "equal volumes."

What you see

End point and indicators

The end point is where the indicator changes color and you stop. A good indicator makes the end point fall within a drop of the equivalence point. Some titrants indicate themselves: purple permanganate turns colorless as it reacts, so the first lasting pink-purple marks the end point.

The calculation

Volume → moles → ratio → molarity

Moles of titrant = M × V (in liters). Use the balanced equation's coefficients to convert to moles of analyte. Divide by the analyte volume in liters. Every step is just stoichiometry from topic 4.5.

Particle view

Reactant gets used up

Before the equivalence point, the analyte is in excess and every added titrant particle reacts. After it, the titrant is in excess and its particles pile up unreacted. Spectator ions only accumulate or stay constant.

Coming in Unit 8

pH curves

For acid–base titrations, pH jumps sharply near the equivalence point, which is why one drop flips the indicator. Unit 8 graphs the whole curve and explains why a weak acid's equivalence point is above pH 7. For Unit 4, focus on the mole bookkeeping.

3. Titration cheat sheet

The same four steps solve every titration problem. Only the mole ratio and the end-point signal change from reaction to reaction.

The four steps

  1. Volume of titrant delivered = final burette reading − initial reading. Convert mL to L.
  2. Moles of titrant = Mtitrant × Vtitrant.
  3. Moles of analyte = moles of titrant × (analyte coefficient ÷ titrant coefficient).
  4. Manalyte = moles of analyte ÷ Vanalyte (in L). Report to the significant figures the data allow, usually four for burette and pipet volumes.

4. Titration on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Write the balanced equation first and point to the coefficients you use for the mole ratio.
  • Show each step with units: "mol NaOH = (0.1000 mol/L)(0.01840 L) = 1.840 × 10−3 mol." Graders want the setup, not just the answer.
  • Define the equivalence point in terms of moles: "moles of titrant added are stoichiometrically equivalent to the moles of analyte initially present."
  • Name the glassware when asked: burette for the titrant, volumetric pipet for the analyte, Erlenmeyer flask to swirl in.
  • For error questions, trace the effect through the math: does the mistake make the recorded titrant volume too large or too small? The calculated analyte amount moves the same way.
  • Keep significant figures consistent with the data (four for 25.00 mL and 0.1000 M).

Common mistakes

  • Using M1V1 = M2V2 when the ratio is not 1 : 1. For H2SO4 with NaOH you need twice as many moles of NaOH.
  • Saying the equivalence point is where "the volumes are equal" or "the pH is 7." Neither is the definition.
  • Forgetting to convert mL to L before multiplying by molarity.
  • Thinking water added to the flask changes the result. It changes the concentration in the flask, not the moles of analyte, so the titrant volume needed is the same.
  • Overshooting the end point. The recorded volume is too large, so the calculated analyte concentration comes out too high.
  • Treating spectator ions as if they react. Na+ and Cl− are in the flask before and after; only H+ and OH− react in HCl + NaOH.
