2. Key ideas

Like dissolves like

Similar IMFs mix

Substances with similar intermolecular interactions tend to be soluble in or miscible with each other. Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents such as water; nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.

Break and make

Three sets of attractions

Dissolving breaks solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions and forms solute–solvent attractions. If the new attractions are comparable in strength to the ones broken, the solute dissolves.

Polar + nonpolar

Why oil and water separate

Hexane can form only weak London forces with water. To dissolve, it would have to break water's strong hydrogen bonds and get little back, so the water molecules stay together and push the hexane out as a separate layer.

Ionic solutes

Ion–dipole attractions

Water's dipoles surround ions and can make up for the strong ionic attractions in the solid. Hexane has no dipole, so ionic solids do not dissolve in it. Not every ionic solid dissolves in water; all sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts do.

Two-part molecules

Polar head, nonpolar tail

Alcohols have a polar −OH and a nonpolar carbon chain. Short chains (methanol, ethanol) mix with water in any proportion. Each added CH2 makes the molecule more like hexane and less soluble in water.

Miscible

Vocabulary

Miscible liquids mix in all proportions to form one phase. Immiscible liquids form separate layers, with the less dense liquid on top. Soluble and insoluble describe a solid or gas dissolving in a liquid.

4. Explaining it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • "Like dissolves like" alone earns little. Name the IMFs of the solute, of the solvent, and between them.
  • Use comparative language: "the London dispersion forces between I2 and hexane are similar in strength to those broken in pure I2 and pure hexane."
  • For ionic solutes in water, say "ion–dipole attractions between the ions and the polar water molecules."
  • For a polar–nonpolar pair, explain that the solute–solvent attractions are too weak to make up for the hydrogen bonds (or dipole attractions) that would be broken.
  • When comparing two similar molecules, point to the structural feature: an −OH group, the length of a nonpolar chain, a dipole.
  • For immiscible liquids, say which layer is on top using density.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a nonpolar substance is "repelled" by water. The issue is that it cannot form attractions strong enough to replace water's hydrogen bonds.
  • Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular solute dissolves. Only intermolecular attractions change.
  • Assuming every ionic compound dissolves in water.
  • Calling any molecule with H and O "hydrogen-bonding." Acetone can accept a hydrogen bond from water but cannot donate one.
  • Forgetting that a molecule can have a polar part and a nonpolar part, and both matter.
  • Using "soluble" for two liquids where "miscible" is the precise word.
