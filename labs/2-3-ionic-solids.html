Structure of Ionic Solids Study Lab

An ionic solid is not a pile of molecules. It is a repeating three-dimensional lattice in which every cation is surrounded by anions and every anion by cations, an arrangement that maximizes attraction and minimizes repulsion. That structure explains why ionic solids melt at high temperatures, shatter instead of bending, and conduct electricity only when the ions can move.

2. Key ideas

Structure

A lattice, not molecules

Cations and anions pack in a repeating 3D pattern in which each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge. In NaCl each Na⁺ has 6 Cl⁻ nearest neighbors and each Cl⁻ has 6 Na⁺. The formula NaCl gives only the 1 : 1 ratio.

Drawing ions

Cations are usually smaller

A cation has lost electrons (often a whole shell), so it is smaller than its neutral atom. An anion has gained electrons, so it is larger. In particle diagrams, draw the anion larger, and keep oppositely charged ions next to each other.

Coulomb's law

Strength of the lattice

The attraction between ions grows with the charges and shrinks with distance (∝ q₁q₂/r). Higher charges and smaller ions give a larger lattice energy and usually a higher melting point. MgO melts above 2800 °C; NaCl at 801 °C.

Brittle

Why ionic crystals shatter

A strong force can shift one layer of ions relative to the next. After a shift of one ion position, cations line up with cations and anions with anions. The like charges repel and the crystal splits along that plane.

Conductivity

Moving ions carry current

Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: the ions are locked in place and there are no free electrons. When melted or dissolved, the ions can move, so the liquid or solution conducts electricity.

Other properties

Low vapor pressure, high melting point

Pulling ions out of the lattice means breaking many strong Coulombic attractions, so ionic solids have very low vapor pressures and high melting and boiling points.

4. Writing it on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Compare ionic compounds with Coulomb's law: name both the charges and the sizes of the specific ions, then link to the strength of attraction.
  • Connect stronger attraction to the property asked about: "more energy is required to separate the ions, so the melting point is higher."
  • In particle diagrams of ionic solids, alternate cations and anions, draw the anions larger (for typical cases), and show no discrete molecules.
  • Explain brittleness with repulsion: shifting a layer brings like-charged ions next to each other.
  • Explain conductivity with mobile charged particles: ions are fixed in the solid but free to move when molten or dissolved.
  • For dissolved ions, draw water molecules with the O end toward cations and the H ends toward anions.

Common mistakes

  • Saying molten NaCl conducts because of free electrons. It conducts because the ions are mobile.
  • Drawing NaCl as separate Na–Cl pairs or molecules. Each ion is surrounded by six ions of the opposite charge.
  • Explaining a melting point difference with "stronger bonds" and nothing else. Say why: larger charges, smaller ions, Coulomb's law.
  • Comparing sizes when the charges differ. Charge usually dominates: CaO beats NaF even though Ca²⁺ is about the same size as Na⁺.
  • Using electronegativity to explain lattice strength. The AP argument is Coulombic: charge and distance.
  • Saying ionic solids bend like metals. Layers can't slide past each other without bringing like charges together.
