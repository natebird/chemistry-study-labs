Photoelectron Spectroscopy Study Lab

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) knocks electrons out of atoms with high-energy photons and measures how much energy it took to remove each one. Every subshell shows up as a peak: its position is the binding energy, and its height is the number of electrons. Generate spectra, compare neighbors, and read them the way the AP exam does.

2. Key ideas

One peak per subshell

What a peak means

Each peak is a group of electrons with the same binding energy, one subshell (1s, 2s, 2p, …). The number of peaks tells you how many occupied subshells the atom has.

Position

Binding energy and Coulomb's law

A peak's position is the energy needed to remove an electron from that subshell. Electrons closer to the nucleus feel a stronger attraction, so 1s is always the leftmost (highest-energy) peak.

Height

Number of electrons

Peak height (or area) is proportional to the number of electrons in the subshell. For sodium the heights are 2 : 2 : 6 : 1 for 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s. Use heights to tell a 2p⁶ peak from a 2p³ peak.

More protons, shift left

Comparing elements

Going from one element to the next, the nuclear charge rises by one, so the same subshell's peak moves to a higher binding energy. The 1s peak of Mg (126 MJ/mol) is to the left of Na's (104 MJ/mol).

Ephoton = BE + KE

How the instrument works

A photon of known energy ejects an electron. The instrument measures the electron's kinetic energy, and binding energy = photon energy − kinetic energy. Tightly bound electrons leave with less kinetic energy.

Evidence for the model

Why PES matters

PES is direct evidence for shells and subshells. The 2s and 2p peaks of boron have different energies, which shows the second shell is split into subshells of different energy.

3. Reading a PES spectrum

Work from left (highest energy, innermost) to right (lowest energy, outermost). Read the heights as electron counts, and you can write the configuration.

4. PES on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Identify each peak by subshell and say how you know: position for which shell, height for how many electrons.
  • When comparing peak positions of two elements, use Coulomb's law: same subshell, more protons, so greater attraction and higher binding energy.
  • When comparing two peaks in one atom, use distance and shielding: the inner subshell is closer to the nucleus and less shielded.
  • Draw a predicted peak in the right place: to the left of the other element's peak for more protons, and with the correct relative height.
  • Connect the rightmost peak to the first ionization energy when a question asks about ionization.
  • Use the photon equation if asked: binding energy = photon energy − kinetic energy of the ejected electron.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the axis the wrong way. On AP spectra binding energy increases to the left.
  • Assigning peaks by height alone. The 1s peak is not the tallest; it is the leftmost.
  • Saying a taller peak is "harder to remove." Height counts electrons; position measures energy.
  • Explaining a left shift with "more shielding" or "more electrons." For the same subshell in the next element, the cause is one more proton.
  • Forgetting that 2s and 2p are separate peaks. Starting with boron, the second shell gives two peaks.
  • Placing the 4s peak of K to the left of the 3p peak. The 4s electron is outermost, so it is the lowest-energy peak.
