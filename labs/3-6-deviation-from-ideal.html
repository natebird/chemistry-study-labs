Deviation from Ideal Gas Law Study Lab

The ideal gas law assumes gas particles take up no space and do not attract each other. Real particles do both, so real gases drift away from PV = nRT at high pressure and low temperature. Squeeze and cool six real gases, read PV/nRT, and see which particle-level effect wins.

1. Squeeze and cool a real gas

The ratio PV/nRT is exactly 1 for an ideal gas. Below 1, attractions are winning; above 1, the size of the particles is winning. The curves are calculated with the van der Waals equation and textbook constants. That equation is beyond the AP exam and is itself only an approximate model, so read these curves for trends, not exact values. Where a curve ends, the model predicts the gas condenses to a liquid.

2. Key ideas

Two assumptions fail

Why real gases deviate

The ideal gas model treats particles as points with no attractions. Real particles have volume and do attract each other through intermolecular forces. When either effect becomes significant, PV/nRT moves away from 1.

PV/nRT < 1

Attractions lower the pressure

A particle about to hit the wall is pulled back slightly by its neighbors, so it strikes with less force. Particles also cluster briefly. The measured pressure is lower than PV = nRT predicts. This dominates at moderate pressures and low temperatures.

PV/nRT > 1

Particle volume raises it

At very high pressure the particles themselves fill a noticeable share of the container. The empty space they can move in is smaller than V, so they hit the walls more often than the ideal model predicts. The measured pressure (or volume) is higher than ideal.

Most ideal

High T, low P

At low pressure the particles are far apart, so their volume is negligible and they rarely feel each other. At high temperature they move so fast that attractions have little effect during a collision. Real gases behave most ideally under these conditions.

Which gas?

Stronger IMFs, bigger deviation

Gases with stronger intermolecular forces (polar molecules, hydrogen bonding, large polarizable electron clouds) deviate more. NH₃ and CO₂ deviate far more than He or H₂, which have tiny, weakly attracting particles.

The extreme case

Condensation

Cool a gas or squeeze it enough and the attractions take over completely: the gas condenses to a liquid. Near that point the ideal gas law fails badly, and once it is a liquid the gas laws no longer apply at all.

4. Deviations on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Say which assumption fails: "particles have attractions" or "particles have volume that is not negligible."
  • Give the direction: attractions make the measured pressure lower than predicted; particle volume makes it higher.
  • Explain with collisions: attractions reduce the force and frequency of wall collisions; particle volume reduces the free space, so collisions are more frequent.
  • Name the conditions: deviations are largest at high pressure and low temperature; gases are most ideal at low pressure and high temperature.
  • When comparing gases, name the intermolecular force (hydrogen bonding in NH₃, dispersion forces from many electrons in CO₂) that makes one less ideal.
  • If a lab result for molar mass or moles is off, connect it: a lower measured P makes calculated n = PV/RT too small.

Common mistakes

  • Saying attractions make the pressure higher. They pull particles back from the wall, so the pressure is lower.
  • Saying gases are most ideal at low temperature. Slow particles feel attractions more; high temperature is more ideal.
  • Saying particle volume matters at low pressure. At 1 atm the particles fill well under 1% of the space.
  • Blaming "collisions are not elastic" for deviations. AP answers use attractions and particle volume.
  • Thinking a nonpolar gas is always nearly ideal. CO₂ has large dispersion forces and deviates strongly.
  • Forgetting that the same gas can show PV/nRT below 1 at moderate pressure and above 1 at very high pressure.
