2. Key ideas

Protons move

Acid–base reactions

An acid–base reaction transfers one or more protons (H+) from an acid to a base. Look for a species that loses an H+ and another that gains one: HCl → Cl−, NH3 → NH4+. No oxidation numbers change.

Electrons move

Redox reactions

A redox reaction transfers one or more electrons. The proof is a change in oxidation number: the species that is oxidized goes up, the species that is reduced goes down, and the electrons lost equal the electrons gained.

A solid forms

Precipitation reactions

Two solutions are mixed and dissolved ions combine to form a product with low solubility, which comes out as a solid. The net ionic equation shows only the ions that form the solid, such as Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s).

Combustion

Burning is redox

Combustion is a redox reaction in which a substance reacts with O2 to form oxides, usually CO2 and H2O for compounds of C and H. Oxygen goes from 0 to −2 (reduced) and carbon's oxidation number rises (oxidized).

Solubility you must know

Na+, K+, NH4+, NO3−

The only solubility rule the AP exam expects you to know: all sodium, potassium, ammonium, and nitrate salts are soluble in water. For any other salt, the question will tell you or show (s) in the equation.

Traps

Look at the evidence, not the names

An acid in the equation does not make it acid–base (Mg + HCl is redox). A solid product does not make it precipitation (Zn + Cu2+ deposits Cu metal by redox). Some reactions are two types: Ba(OH)2 + H2SO4 is acid–base and precipitation.

4. Classifying on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the type and give the evidence in the same sentence. "Redox" alone rarely earns the point; "redox, because the oxidation number of Mg increases from 0 to +2" does.
  • For redox, name both halves: what is oxidized (and its oxidation numbers before and after) and what is reduced.
  • For acid–base, name the proton donor and the proton acceptor.
  • For precipitation, name the solid and the two aqueous ions that formed it, and write the net ionic equation.
  • When asked about combustion, say it is redox and point to O2 (0) becoming oxygen at −2 in CO2 and H2O.
  • Check that your evidence is about particles (protons, electrons, ions), not observations alone like "bubbles formed."

Common mistakes

  • Calling Mg + HCl acid–base because HCl is an acid. H+ gains electrons to become H2; that is reduction.
  • Calling a reaction precipitation because a metal forms. Cu(s) from Zn + Cu2+ is made by electron transfer.
  • Calling CaCO3 + HCl redox because a gas forms. No oxidation number changes; carbonate accepts protons.
  • Forgetting that a free element (Zn, O2, Cl2) on one side and in a compound on the other almost always means redox.
  • Assuming every ionic product is a precipitate. Sodium, potassium, ammonium, and nitrate salts always stay dissolved.
  • Stopping at one type. Check all three questions; some reactions fit two.
