Buffer Capacity Study Lab

Two buffers can have exactly the same pH and very different staying power. The ratio of conjugate base to acid sets the pH; the amounts of each set how much added acid or base the buffer can absorb before the pH swings. Race two acetate buffers against strong acid and strong base and watch which one runs out first.

Bigger capacity

pH values solve the full equilibrium with Ka(CH3COOH) = 1.8 × 10−5 at 25 °C. The one-unit definition of capacity is just a convenient yardstick for this lab. The CED asks you to explain capacity from the amounts of the two components, so compare these numbers rather than memorizing them.

2. Key ideas

Definition

Buffer capacity

The amount of strong acid or strong base a buffer can neutralize before its pH changes significantly. It depends on the moles of each component present, not just on the pH.

Same ratio, more stuff

Concentration sets capacity

A 1.0 M / 1.0 M acetate buffer and a 0.10 M / 0.10 M acetate buffer have the same pH (4.74), because the ratio is 1 in both. The concentrated one has ten times as much of each component, so it can absorb about ten times as much acid or base.

Unequal amounts

Capacity is lopsided

Added base is consumed by the acid member; added acid by the base member. A buffer with more CH3COOH than CH3COO− has more capacity for added base than for added acid, and the reverse.

Best balance

Equal amounts

For a fixed total amount of the pair, capacity is most balanced when [HA] = [A−], which is when pH = pKa. The buffer then resists acid and base equally well.

Running out

Exceeding capacity

Once the reacting component is nearly used up, added H3O+ or OH− has little left to react with and builds up, and the pH changes sharply, just like near an equivalence point on a titration curve.

Dilution

Same pH, less capacity

Adding water keeps the ratio and the pH but lowers the concentration of both components, so each liter of the buffer can absorb less before it is overwhelmed.

4. Buffer capacity on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Separate the two ideas: "the ratio [A−]/[HA] determines the pH; the amounts of A− and HA determine the capacity."
  • Name which component reacts: added acid is consumed by the conjugate base, so capacity for acid depends on moles of A−.
  • Compare moles, not concentrations, when the buffers have different volumes.
  • When a buffer's capacity is exceeded, say the reacting component has been used up and the excess strong acid or base now determines the pH.
  • To design a high-capacity buffer at a given pH, keep the ratio and raise both concentrations.
  • In a lab-error question, explain how a mistake in amounts changes capacity even if the pH is unchanged.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a more concentrated buffer has a lower (or higher) pH. If the ratio is the same, the pH is the same.
  • Getting the lopsided case backward: more HA means more capacity for added base.
  • Thinking capacity depends on Ka alone. Two acetate buffers share a Ka and can still differ tenfold in capacity.
  • Saying a buffer "stops working" the moment any acid is added. It works until the reacting component is nearly gone.
  • Saying dilution changes the pH of a buffer. It changes the capacity.
  • Picking the buffer with the largest total concentration when the question asks about one direction. Look at the component that reacts.
