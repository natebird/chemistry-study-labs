Introduction to Rate Law Study Lab

A rate law, rate = k[A]m[B]n, says how the rate depends on each reactant's concentration. The orders m and n come from experiments, not from the balanced equation. Here you run initial-rate trials, compare pairs of trials to find each order, and confirm your rate law by checking that every trial gives the same rate constant.

2. Key ideas

The equation

Rate = k[A]m[B]n

The rate is proportional to each reactant's concentration raised to a power. The power is the order with respect to that reactant; the sum m + n is the overall order. Products and catalysts can appear in some rate laws, but the AP course works mostly with reactants.

From experiment

Orders are measured

For an overall reaction, the orders do not come from the coefficients. 2NO2 + F2 → 2NO2F has rate = k[NO2][F2], not k[NO2]2[F2]. Only for an elementary step can you read the rate law from the equation (topic 5.4).

Method

Compare initial rates

Pick two trials in which only [A] changes. Then rate2/rate1 = ([A]2/[A]1)m. Doubling [A] and seeing the rate double gives m = 1; quadruple gives m = 2; no change gives m = 0.

What orders mean

0, 1, 2

Zero order: changing that concentration has no effect on rate. First order: rate changes by the same factor as the concentration. Second order: rate changes by the square of the factor (×3 in concentration gives ×9 in rate).

Rate constant

k depends on temperature

k is the same for every trial at one temperature; it does not change with concentration or as the reaction runs. Raising the temperature increases k. Its units depend on the overall order, so that k × (M)order gives M/s.

In the lab

Watching the rate

Follow a property that tracks concentration. In the crystal violet investigation, the purple dye fades as it reacts with OH−; absorbance is proportional to concentration (A = εbc), so absorbance readings over time give the concentration of dye over time.

Solving an initial-rates table

  1. Find two trials where only [A] changes. Write rate2/rate1 = ([A]2/[A]1)m and solve for m (usually 0, 1, or 2).
  2. Do the same for B with a pair in which only [B] changes.
  3. If no pair isolates a reactant, use the order you already know to cancel its effect, then solve for the other.
  4. Write rate = k[A]m[B]n. Substitute any one trial to solve for k, and give k its units from the overall order.

4. Rate laws on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Name the trials you compare and say what is held constant: "In trials 1 and 2, [O2] is constant while [NO] doubles."
  • State the result as a ratio: "the rate increases by a factor of 4, so the reaction is second order in NO."
  • Write the full rate law with k, using square brackets for concentration.
  • Show the substitution for k with one trial's numbers and give correct units (M−2 s−1 for third order overall).
  • When asked how a change affects the rate, use the rate law: "rate = k[NO]2[O2], so tripling [NO] multiplies the rate by 9."
  • When asked about k, say that it changes only with temperature (or a catalyst), not with concentration.

Common mistakes

  • Taking orders from the coefficients of an overall equation.
  • Comparing two trials in which both concentrations changed, and assigning the whole rate change to one reactant.
  • Forgetting that zero order is possible: if the rate does not change, the order is 0 and the reactant drops out of the rate law.
  • Giving k the wrong units, or no units.
  • Saying k gets larger when concentration increases. The rate gets larger; k stays the same.
  • Putting the products' concentrations on top, as in an equilibrium expression. A rate law is not K.
