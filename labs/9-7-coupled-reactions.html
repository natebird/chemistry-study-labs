Coupled Reactions Study Lab

A reaction with ΔG° > 0 is not thermodynamically favored, but it can still be made to happen. Either an outside source of energy (electricity, light) pushes it, or it is paired with a strongly favored reaction that shares an intermediate, so the sum of the two has ΔG° < 0. Build those pairings below and read the free-energy ledger.

2. Key ideas

Starting point

Unfavored is not impossible

ΔG° > 0 means the reaction is not thermodynamically favored on its own (K < 1, so at equilibrium reactants dominate). It can still be made to go if something supplies the free energy it lacks.

Outside energy

Electricity and light

An external energy source can drive an unfavored process. Electrical energy runs an electrolytic cell or recharges a battery. Light absorbed by chlorophyll drives the overall conversion of CO2 and H2O into glucose and O2 in photosynthesis.

Coupling

Add the equations, add the ΔG° values

Pair the unfavored reaction with a favored one. Because the overall equation is the sum of the two, the overall ΔG° is the sum of their ΔG° values. If that sum is negative, the overall process is thermodynamically favored and the desired product forms.

The link

A common intermediate

Coupling only works chemically if the reactions share a species: one makes it and the other uses it up. That shared species (Pi for ATP, O2 for smelting) cancels when the equations are added. Two reactions that merely happen in the same flask are not coupled.

Biology

ATP → ADP + Pi

Cells use ATP hydrolysis (about −30.5 kJ/mol) to drive uphill reactions such as attaching phosphate to glucose (+13.8 kJ/mol). The phosphate group passes through the shared intermediate, and the sum is about −16.7 kJ/mol.

Scaling

Multiply to match

If one copy of the helper is not enough, or the intermediate does not cancel, multiply the helper equation. ΔG° scales with the coefficients (doubling the equation doubles ΔG°). Cell potential E° does not scale this way; you will see why in topic 9.9.

Coupling two reactions, step by step

  1. Write the unfavored reaction (ΔG°1 > 0) and the favored helper (ΔG°2 < 0).
  2. Find the species made in one and used up in the other: the common intermediate.
  3. Multiply the helper by whatever factor makes the intermediate cancel; multiply its ΔG° by the same factor.
  4. Add the equations, cancel the intermediate, and add the ΔG° values: ΔG°overall = ΔG°1 + m·ΔG°2.
  5. If ΔG°overall < 0, the coupled process is thermodynamically favored (K > 1).

4. Coupled reactions on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • Show the addition explicitly: "ΔG°overall = (+13.8 kJ/mol) + (−30.5 kJ/mol) = −16.7 kJ/mol."
  • Connect the sign to the claim: "Because ΔG°overall < 0, the coupled process is thermodynamically favored."
  • Name the common intermediate and say it is produced in one reaction and consumed in the other.
  • When an equation is multiplied, multiply its ΔG° by the same factor and say so.
  • For electrolysis, recharging, or photosynthesis, name the outside energy source (electrical energy, light) that drives the unfavored process.
  • Keep units and signs on every value, in kJ/molrxn.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the favored reaction "gives off heat that the other one absorbs." Coupling is about adding free energy changes through a shared intermediate, not transferring heat.
  • Saying coupling changes ΔG° of the unfavored reaction. It does not; ΔG°1 is still positive. The overall reaction is a different reaction with its own ΔG°.
  • Forgetting to multiply ΔG° when the helper equation is multiplied to cancel the intermediate.
  • Calling a reaction with ΔG° > 0 "impossible." It is not favored on its own; outside energy or coupling can make it happen.
  • Confusing thermodynamics with kinetics: a catalyst speeds a reaction up but never makes ΔG° negative.
  • Adding magnitudes instead of signed values (13.8 + 30.5 = 44.3 is not the overall ΔG°).
