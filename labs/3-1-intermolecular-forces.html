Intermolecular and Interparticle Forces Study Lab

Intermolecular forces are the attractions between separate molecules, and they decide boiling points, melting points, and vapor pressures. Every molecule has London dispersion forces; polar molecules add dipole–dipole forces; molecules with H bonded to N, O, or F can hydrogen bond. Inspect each force at the particle level, then rank real boiling points and check your reasoning.

2. Key ideas

Every molecule

London dispersion forces

Electrons are always moving, so at any instant a molecule can have a temporary dipole that induces a dipole in its neighbor. The attraction is stronger when the electron cloud is more polarizable: more electrons, larger atoms, and more surface contact between molecules.

Polar molecules

Dipole–dipole forces

A molecule with a permanent dipole lines up its δ+ end with the δ− end of a neighbor. Polarity needs polar bonds and a shape where the bond dipoles do not cancel. CO2 and CCl4 have polar bonds but are nonpolar.

H bonded to N, O, F

Hydrogen bonding

An especially strong dipole–dipole attraction between an H atom covalently bonded to N, O, or F and a lone pair on an N, O, or F atom of another molecule. It explains why water, HF, and alcohols boil far higher than similar-size molecules.

Ions in polar solvents

Ion–dipole forces

An ion attracts the oppositely charged end of polar molecules: the O side of water around a cation, the H side around an anion. These attractions are why many ionic compounds dissolve in water. A polar molecule can also induce a dipole in a nonpolar one (dipole–induced dipole).

Size can win

Dispersion can beat dipoles

Do not assume polar always boils higher. HI is less polar than HCl but boils higher because its 54 electrons give much larger dispersion forces. CCl4 (nonpolar) boils far above CH3F (polar). Compare dispersion forces whenever sizes differ a lot.

Intra vs. inter

Bonds don't break on boiling

Covalent bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule; intermolecular forces act between molecules and are much weaker. Boiling water separates H2O molecules from each other; it does not break O–H bonds.

4. IMFs on the FRQ

What earns points

  • Name the specific force for each substance being compared. "Stronger IMFs" alone does not earn the point.
  • For dispersion forces, connect electrons to polarizability: "I2 has more electrons, so its electron cloud is more polarizable and its dispersion forces are stronger."
  • For hydrogen bonding, say where it comes from: an H atom bonded to O (or N, F) attracted to a lone pair on O of a neighboring molecule.
  • Tie the force to the property: stronger IMFs mean more energy needed to separate molecules, so a higher boiling point and lower vapor pressure.
  • When the molecules are similar in size, compare polarity and hydrogen bonding; when sizes are very different, address dispersion forces first.
  • In a diagram, draw a hydrogen bond as a dashed line from H on one molecule to a lone pair on N, O, or F of another.

Common mistakes

  • Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils.
  • Counting any H atom as a hydrogen-bond donor. In CH3OCH3 and CH3CHO every H is on carbon, so these molecules do not hydrogen bond with each other.
  • Drawing a hydrogen bond inside one molecule, along the O–H covalent bond.
  • Using molar mass as the explanation. Mass correlates with electrons, but the reason is polarizability of the electron cloud.
  • Assuming polar always beats nonpolar. Large nonpolar molecules can have stronger total IMFs than small polar ones.
  • Calling a molecule polar because it has polar bonds. Check the shape: symmetric molecules like CO2 and CH4 are nonpolar.
