Representations of Equilibrium Study Lab

The same equilibrium can be shown as a particle diagram, a graph of concentration against time, or a graph of forward and reverse rates. Each one tells you whether the system has reached equilibrium and, with counts, the value of K. Here you run a reaction in a sealed box and read all three pictures side by side, then pick out the diagram that is at equilibrium.

2. Key ideas

Particle diagrams

Counts become concentrations

Divide each count by the volume (or use the stated amount per particle) to get concentrations, then substitute into the K expression with the coefficients as exponents. The diagram is at equilibrium only if that Q equals K.

Equilibrium is not equal amounts

Constant, not equal

At equilibrium the amounts stop changing. They are almost never equal to each other. Equal amounts of reactant and product would only match K for special cases such as A ⇌ B with K = 1.

Concentration–time

Look for the plateau

Reactant curves fall and product curves rise, steeply at first, then level off. The time at which every curve is flat is when equilibrium is reached. The changes are in the ratio of the coefficients: for 2NO2 ⇌ N2O4, [NO2] falls twice as fast as [N2O4] rises.

Rate–time

Two curves that meet

Starting with only reactants, the forward rate starts high and falls while the reverse rate starts at zero and rises. Where they meet and stay equal, the system is at equilibrium. Neither rate falls to zero.

Dynamic

Particles keep reacting

A particle diagram at equilibrium is a snapshot. A moment later different particles may have reacted, but just as many form as are used up, so the counts are the same.

Either direction

Same K from either side

At a given temperature, a mixture made from reactants and one made from the equivalent amount of products reach the same equilibrium composition. One gets there with Q rising to K, the other with Q falling to K.

The same three questions apply to every picture: is it at equilibrium, which way is it moving, and what does it say about K?

4. Representations on the FRQ

What earns points on the FRQ

  • When you draw a particle diagram, conserve atoms: count every atom of each element in the starting box and make the equilibrium box match.
  • Make the counts in an equilibrium drawing satisfy K; show the Q calculation that proves it.
  • On a concentration–time sketch, make the changes match the coefficients and draw every curve flat after the same time.
  • On a rate–time sketch, start the reverse rate at zero (if no product is present) and have the two curves meet at a nonzero value.
  • Justify "at equilibrium" with a measurable reason: the concentrations are constant, or Q = K, or the forward and reverse rates are equal.
  • Include the legend: say what each kind of particle represents.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the point where two concentration curves cross the equilibrium point. Crossing only means the two amounts are momentarily equal.
  • Drawing equal numbers of reactant and product particles "because it is at equilibrium."
  • Showing the forward rate dropping to zero, as if the reaction stopped.
  • Forgetting the exponent from the coefficient when calculating K from counts (for 2AB the count is squared).
  • Losing or gaining atoms between the starting and equilibrium diagrams.
  • Thinking a reaction started from products reaches a different equilibrium mixture than one started from the equivalent reactants.
